Portie

No Root v1.0.3 By Sol AI November 2026 ~15 min read

Portie is a native macOS network scanner. Point it at a host, pick a port range, and it tells you which doors are open and what they are — then, if you want, it shakes hands with the encrypted ones and reads their certificates. No sudo, no entitlements, no Electron, no Python venv. Just SwiftUI and Apple's Network framework. MIT licensed, source on GitHub.

⚠️ Read this first

What is Portie?

Portie is a small native macOS app that scans a host for open TCP ports and reports what it finds. Every computer on a network has thousands of numbered doors — one serves web pages, one serves mail, one is a remote login. Portie walks up to each one, knocks, listens, and writes down what answered.

It is built with SwiftUI for the interface and Apple's Network framework for the connections. That last part matters: NWConnection needs no raw sockets, so Portie runs as a normal user process. No sudo, no com.apple.security.network.client entitlement, no Electron bundle eating 200 MB of RAM.

There is also a Decrypt action: for every open port, Portie performs a real TLS handshake and tells you the negotiated protocol version, the cipher suite, and who issued the certificate sitting on the other end. That turns "port 443 is open" into "port 443 is open, serving a Let's Encrypt cert for example.com, expiring in March".

🔍 Async TCP Scan

Any inclusive port range, from 1–1024 up to 1–65535, probed concurrently over NWConnection. No root, no entitlements, no Electron.

⚡ Tunable Speed

Per-port timeout and parallel worker count are both adjustable. The worker pool starts at 200 and scales with the range you pick.

🖥️ Live Terminal

Built-in monospaced terminal with timestamped lines and colour-coded severity: info, success, error, banner, and warnings.

🏷️ Service Hints

Open ports come back with a friendly name attached — SSH, HTTP, MySQL, Redis — plus a bundled 93-entry port database with transport, encryption type, and risk notes.

🔐 Decrypt (TLS)

A real TLS handshake on every open port: version, cipher suite, subject, issuer, SAN list, validity window, serial number, signature algorithm.

🔧 Settings Panel

Minimum TLS version, SNI host override, certificate verification toggle, handshake timeout, and a show-cipher switch — all under ⌘,.

📄 One-Click Reports

Save the full transcript to your Desktop as a plain-text Portie-<host>-<timestamp>.txt file, and reveal it in Finder.

🧩 No Lock-In

MIT licensed, Swift Package Manager layout, ad-hoc codesigned .app bundle, and a command-line companion binary.

Why native?

A GUI scanner that needs sudo to open a socket is a bad scanner. By using Network.framework and Apple's security APIs directly, Portie keeps the privilege surface at zero — which is also why it installs by dropping a folder on your Desktop and opening it.

Safety & authorisation

⚠️ The one rule

Scan only what you own or have written permission to test. Being able to reach a system is not permission. Neither is being on the same network as it, and neither is having a login of your own on part of it — unless that login explicitly includes the rest. Portie connects from your real network identity: there is no IP spoofing, so the target will see you coming.

Portie's own learning material states the doctrine plainly, and this page follows it exactly. Here is the rule set, condensed:

  • Ownership or written permission, always. Your own laptop, your own router, your own home server — fine. Someone else's system — only with permission that exists in writing. A signed statement of authorisation is the gold standard.
  • The default posture is read-only. Observation is the safest useful activity: connect, ask the standard questions, record what comes back. Anything that attempts a login, trips alarms, or alters configuration is outside the boundary.
  • Scope means something specific. Which systems (addresses, ranges, domains), which dates, which techniques, and who to contact if something breaks. If any of those are blank, you do not have a scope.
  • Stop when the system pushes back. Lockouts, bans, rate-limit warnings, monitoring notices, a degrading service, or a honeypot warning all mean stop and reassess — not push harder.
  • Findings are someone else's secrets. Report it, don't collect more than needed to prove the point, and keep real credentials and personal data out of your tooling and out of chat.
  • The line you do not cross. You do not use Portie to obtain access to a system you are not authorised to test, and you do not use it to obtain, crack, or use credentials belonging to anyone else.

⚠️ Capability is not permission

Portie's engine is powerful, and some of that power sits outside the observation-only boundary described above (for example, credential-recovery workflows). Their existence does not make them appropriate. The rules in this section apply to them with full force: authorise first, scope it, observe, report.

Pre-flight checklist

Print this. Complete it before any scan that leaves your own machine.

  • The target belongs to me, or I hold written permission to test it.
  • The scope names specific addresses or ranges — and I have checked no neighbouring or third-party system falls inside them.
  • The scope names dates, and today is inside them.
  • I know who to contact if something breaks, and I know when I will stop.
  • I am starting with observation — scan and certificate inspection — and nothing that attempts access to a service.
  • I know where results and transcripts will be stored, and that location suits the sensitivity of the findings.

Start on yourself

For your first ever scan, target 127.0.0.1 — the loopback address that always means "this machine". It never leaves the machine, you cannot lock yourself out of your own laptop, and it is where every Portie tutorial starts.

Installation

Portie ships as source you build yourself — it is a Swift package, and build.sh turns it into a proper, ad-hoc codesigned .app bundle.

Build it

# Clone the repo
git clone https://github.com/TheSolai/portie.git
cd portie

# Build the .app bundle (release build)
./build.sh

# Launch it
open dist/PortieForcie.app

build.sh reads the shipped version from the VERSION file (currently 1.0.3), runs a release swift build, assembles dist/PortieForcie.app with the right Info.plist (display name Portie, bundle id app.portie.forcie.macos), and ad-hoc codesigns it. There is no installer and nothing is written outside the repo.

It lands on your Desktop too

After the build, a copy of the app is placed at ~/Desktop/PortieForcie.app so it sits with your other daily tools. Set SKIP_DESKTOP=1 in the environment to skip that copy entirely. Older bundles in dist/ are named Portie.app; the current build script names the bundle after the executable, PortieForcie.app, and the Finder display name is still Portie.

# Build without touching your Desktop
SKIP_DESKTOP=1 ./build.sh

# Build with extra flags (e.g. verbose Swift output)
SWIFT_FLAGS="--verbose" ./build.sh

Gatekeeper

The bundle is ad-hoc signed rather than signed with an Apple Developer ID, so macOS will greet it with "cannot be opened because the developer cannot be verified" the first time. Right-click the app → Open → confirm. You only do this once per machine.

Which binary am I running?

There are two executables in the package. The GUI is PortieForcie (displayed as "Portie"). The command-line companion is portie, built from the PortieCli target — it exposes the credential-recovery engine from the terminal, not the scanner itself. The scanning engine ships as a separate portie engine binary alongside the app.

First Launch

The window opens with a single row of controls at the top and a large black terminal pane underneath. The terminal is the whole point of the app — every probe, every result, every error is a timestamped line in there.

Portie — native macOS network scanner 
┌─ Scan row ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  Host [127.0.0.1▁]  Start [1]  End [1024]  Timeout [600]   [TCP ▾]  │
│                                                  [▶ Scan] [⚙] [⋯]   │
├─ Terminal ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ [09:14:02] $ portie scan 127.0.0.1 --ports 1-1024 --transport tcp …  │
│ [09:14:02] waiting for probes…                                    │
│ [09:14:03]   + open  127.0.0.1:22                                  │
│ [09:14:03] done in 1.43s — 2 open, 1022 closed/refused               │
└───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

The four fields

  • Host — an IP address or a hostname. Defaults to 127.0.0.1 (your own machine). The field uses a monospaced font because IPs look wrong in anything else.
  • Start / End — an inclusive port range. Defaults to 1–1024, the well-known range.
  • Timeout — milliseconds allowed per probe. Defaults to 600 ms.
  • Transport — a segmented picker for TCP, UDP, or Both. TCP is the safe default; UDP results are inherently ambiguous (see the Troubleshooting section).

The scan is always observation

A scan knocks on a port and records whether the door answered. It does not log in, does not send credentials, and does not change anything on the far side. Keep it that way — that is the posture Portie's own safety doctrine asks for.

Running your first scan

Set your fields, then press Scan (⌘R). Watch the terminal.

What a scan looks like

Portie — terminal output 
[10:42:18] Portie — TCP connect scan
[10:42:18] target  : 127.0.0.1
[10:42:18] range   : 1-1024  (total 1024)
[10:42:18] eta     : 1s – 4s
[10:42:19]   + open  127.0.0.1:22
[10:42:19]   + open  127.0.0.1:631
[10:42:19] done in 1.43s — 2 open, 1022 closed/refused
[10:42:19] OPEN PORTS on 127.0.0.1
[10:42:19] [CRITICAL]  22    TCP  ssh              access 70%  🔑=yes  ⚠anon=no
[10:42:19] [HIGH]      631   TCP  IPP/CUPS         access 60%  🔑=no   ⚠anon=yes
  1. Banner lines open the scan: target, range, total ports, and an ETA.
  2. + open lines appear the moment each port answers. They stream in as results land, not in port order.
  3. The done in line closes the scan with the totals: how long it took, how many ports were open, how many were closed or refused.
  4. The OPEN PORTS table ranks what it found by priority.

Tuning it

  • Timeout — the per-probe budget. The app clamps it to 50–10,000 ms; the shipped engine documents its supported probe range as 50–5,000 ms. Raising it finds slow or filtered hosts but makes the scan slower. Lowering it risks missing genuinely open services.
  • Parallel workers — how many probes are in flight at once. Defaults to 200 and is clamped to 1–2,048 in the app (the engine documents 1–2,000). Raise it only as far as the target can comfortably tolerate: an overloaded little device is a real way to cause harm with a "harmless" scan.
  • Range — 1–1024 covers the services that are most interesting and most common. Widening to 65535 finds more, and takes proportionally longer.

How long will it take?

The settings popover carries a live Estimated scan time strip that recomputes as you change the range, the worker count, and the timeout. A full 1–65535 sweep at the default settings takes a few seconds on a local target; over the internet, budget for latency on every probe.

Stopping a scan

Hit Stop (⌘.) at any point. The button swaps to a red Stop while a run is in flight, and the partial results stay in the terminal so you can read what came back before you killed it.

Reading the terminal output

The terminal is a live transcript, capped at 5,000 lines, with each line stamped with the wall-clock time it was produced. Every line carries a severity, and every severity has a colour.

Severity Colour Means
info white Normal information — Portie telling you what it is about to do.
success green An open port was found, or a TLS handshake completed.
warning yellow / orange Something was unexpected — a server replied with a banner that did not match the port, for example.
error red Failure — the port was closed, refused, or timed out.
banner accent The header of a command, or the summary that closes it.

The open-port table

When the scan finishes, Portie prints the open ports with a priority tag and a few signals:

  • CRITICAL / HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW — how exposed that port is in practice. CRITICAL means fix it today; LOW is informational.
  • TCP / UDP — the transport that answered.
  • Service name — from Portie's service-hint table, which covers the common ports: FTP, SSH, Telnet, SMTP, DNS, HTTP, POP3, IMAP, HTTPS, SMB, Submission, IMAPS, POP3S, MSSQL, MySQL, RDP, PostgreSQL, Redis, HTTP-Alt, HTTPS-Alt, Prometheus, MongoDB, and more.
  • access % — how easy the port is to reach in practice. Higher means more exposed.
  • 🔑=yes — the service requires a password. ⚠anon=yes — it accepts anonymous connections, no password needed. Both of those deserve a second look.

An open port is not a finding

A service being open is completely normal. The question is whether it should be reachable from where you are sitting. Report the gap between expected and actual — not "port 22 was open".

The port database

Behind the service hints sits a bundled database of 93 well-known ports, each with its transport (TCP / UDP / both), its encryption type (plaintext, TLS, STARTTLS-capable, DTLS, SSH-protocol, IPsec, QUIC), and a human risk note where one is warranted. You can browse it in-app with ⌘⇧F (Port Finder) or with the /find slash command — /find ssh turns a number into a name and a paragraph of context.

The Decrypt action

"Decrypt" is Portie's word for the thing a browser does when it visits https://: a TLS handshake. Press Decrypt (⌘D) and Portie performs that handshake on every open port from your last scan, then reports what the server negotiated.

Portie — decrypt output 
[10:43:01]   + 127.0.0.1:443   TLS 1.3  example.com
[10:43:01]         issued by : Let's Encrypt R3
[10:43:01]         SAN       : DNS:example.com, DNS:*.example.com
[10:43:01]         validity  : Mar 5, 2026 → Jun 5, 2026
[10:43:01]         cipher    : 0x1301

What you get back

  • Negotiated TLS version — TLS 1.0 / 1.1 / 1.2 / 1.3. If you see 1.0 or 1.1, that is old and worth flagging.
  • Cipher suite — the negotiated cipher, e.g. 0x1301 for TLS_AES_128_GCM_SHA256. Can be hidden via the show-cipher setting.
  • Certificate subject — who the certificate says it is for.
  • Issuer — who signed it (e.g. Let's Encrypt R3).
  • SAN list — every subject alternative name: DNS:example.com, DNS:*.example.com.
  • Validity window — not-before and not-after dates.
  • Serial number and signature algorithm.
  • Self-signed flag — whether the certificate vouches for itself.

Why this matters

"Port 443 is open" is a fact. "Port 443 is serving a certificate for example.com that expired two months ago, signed by an internal CA nobody has heard of" is a finding. Decrypt is where a port list turns into an audit.

Probing self-signed endpoints

Plenty of internal services use self-signed or private-CA certificates. By default Portie verifies certificates, so those handshakes will be rejected and you will learn nothing. Turn off certificate verification in Settings (⌘,) to probe them anyway — the handshake completes and you still get the full certificate details.

Verification off is a diagnostic, not a habit

Disabling verification means you no longer trust the answer. Use it deliberately to inspect a known endpoint you already own, then turn it back on. Leaving it off in a general scan quietly trains you to ignore the warnings that matter.

Beyond plain TLS

  • Upgrade (⇧⌘D) — tries STARTTLS on services that only speak plaintext until asked to upgrade: SMTP (25/587), IMAP (143), POP3 (110), FTP (21), XMPP (5222), LDAP (389), Postgres (5432).
  • Methods (⌘M) — opens a panel of 10 independent decryption probes you can run selectively or all at once: Direct TLS, STARTTLS, QUIC Initial, DTLS, mTLS request, OCSP stapling, ALPN negotiation, SSH banner + kex, Signed Certificate Timestamp, and a cipher & group audit.

Settings reference

Open Settings with the gear icon or ⌘,. Every control is backed by @AppStorage, so your choices survive quitting and relaunching — they live in ~/Library/Preferences like any other macOS app's settings. There is a Reset button at the top of the panel.

Setting Default What it does
Minimum TLS version TLS 1.2 Floor for the handshake: 1.0, 1.1, 1.2, or 1.3. The maximum is pinned at 1.3, so a server cannot drag the negotiation down to something unexpectedly old.
SNI host override empty (uses the scanned host) Overrides the server name sent in the TLS handshake. Set it when you are probing a virtual host that does not match the address you dialled.
Verify certificates on When off, the handshake completes even for self-signed or untrusted leaves — useful for probing internal endpoints, dangerous as a habit.
Handshake timeout 4,000 ms How long to wait for a TLS handshake to finish. Clamped to 500–20,000 ms.
Show cipher on Whether the negotiated cipher suite appears in the results.

Other scan-side settings

  • Per-probe timeout — the scan's per-port budget, clamped to 50–10,000 ms.
  • Transport — TCP, UDP, or Both. UDP verdicts are a "do not know" by nature; a UDP port that sends no ICMP port-unreachable is reported as open|filtered rather than proven open.
  • Report format — Markdown (default), JSON, or HTML for the written-up export.
  • Include closed ports — whether closed ports appear in the exported report.

Where do the settings live?

~/Library/Preferences/app.portie.forcie.macos.plist — the standard macOS UserDefaults location for the bundle id. Reset them from the Settings panel rather than by hand.

Saving a report

Press ⌘S (or the Save button in the footer) and Portie writes the whole session to your Desktop as a plain-text file, then reveals it in Finder. 

~/Desktop/Portie-127.0.0.1-20261105-104218.txt

The file name is Portie-<host>-<timestamp>.txt, where the timestamp is yyyyMMdd-HHmmss and the host has / and : replaced with underscores so it is always a legal filename.

What is inside

open count   : 2
closed count : 1022

OPEN PORTS
----------

TERMINAL TRANSCRIPT
-------------------
[10:42:18] Portie — TCP connect scan
…

Three parts: the header (target, port range, start time, counts), the open-port list with a service hint beside each number, and the full terminal transcript. Plain UTF-8 text — no format to fight with, no tool to install.

Where do reports end up?

Your Desktop, in plain text, unencrypted, world-readable by anyone with access to your account. Before you share one, check it for anything you would not paste into chat — and delete working copies when the engagement ends.

Also in the footer

  • Export (⌘E) — writes a full written-up penetration report to the Desktop, in Markdown by default, with a plain-English executive summary on top and the technical detail underneath.
  • Reveal Desktop in Finder — File menu shortcut for when you want to see where all those files went.

A note on ⌘K

Some older Portie documentation lists ⌘K as "clear terminal". In the current build ⌘K opens the command palette and ⇧⌘K clears the terminal. The table above matches the shipped menus.

Slash commands

Below the terminal sits a command bar. Type / and a suggestion list appears. The useful ones:

  • /scan <host> — scan a specific host
  • /decrypt — TLS handshake on every open port
  • /upgrade — STARTTLS attempt on every open port
  • /methods — run all decryption probes
  • /probe host:port — knock on a single port
  • /find ssh — search the port database
  • /devices — discover devices on the local network
  • /export — write the written-up report to the Desktop
  • /clear — clear the terminal
  • /help, /settings, /docs, /quit

Troubleshooting

"cannot be opened because the developer cannot be verified"

The bundle is ad-hoc signed, not signed with a Developer ID. Right-click PortieForcie.app → Open → confirm. If it still refuses: System Settings → Privacy & Security → scroll down → Open Anyway. Once per machine.

"scan: PortieGo binary missing"

The GUI shells out to the portie engine binary for scans, and that binary has to be present next to the app. The app's footer menu lists the install locations it probed. If it cannot find it, put the engine binary where the app expects it and relaunch.

Every port comes back closed

  • Timeout too low. A 600 ms probe is fine on a LAN and tight across the internet. Raise it toward 2,000 ms and try again.
  • Wrong transport. If you are scanning UDP, remember UDP verdicts are ambiguous by nature.
  • A firewall is doing its job. Unsolicited probes are supposed to be dropped. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
  • Wrong host. Check for a typo, and check whether the machine answers on a different address.

Decrypt finds nothing on a port you know is HTTPS

  • Minimum TLS version too high. If the server only speaks TLS 1.0, drop the floor in Settings.
  • Certificate verification is rejecting the handshake. For a self-signed endpoint, turn verification off temporarily.
  • Wrong SNI. A virtual host on a shared IP will serve the wrong certificate — or refuse — unless the SNI override names it.
  • It is not implicit TLS. A port like 587 speaks plaintext first and upgrades later. That is what Upgrade (⇧⌘D) is for.
  • Handshake timeout too short for a slow endpoint. The ceiling is 20,000 ms.

The scan feels slow

  • Check the Estimated scan time strip in Settings — it recomputes live as you change the range, worker count, and timeout.
  • Narrow the range. 1–1024 covers the interesting services; 1–65535 covers everything, including the 60,000 ports nobody has ever heard of.
  • Raise the worker count if the target is a machine you own and can take the load.

UDP results look contradictory

A UDP port that sends no ICMP port-unreachable comes back as open|filtered — Portie reports it separately rather than claiming it is open. That ambiguity is inherent to UDP, not a bug in the app.

Reset everything

# Reset the saved settings (the Reset button in Settings does the same)
defaults delete app.portie.forcie.macos

# Rebuild from scratch
cd /path/to/portie
rm -rf .build dist
./build.sh

Before you go wider

If the fix is "scan more ports, faster, with more workers" — pause and re-read the authorisation rules in Safety & authorisation. A wider scan is still a scan, and it still needs a reason.

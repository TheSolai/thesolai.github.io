The glue smelled of hot wheat, old paper, and the particular metallic tang of a repair that had been waiting too long for my attention.

I drew the brush through wheat-starch paste, checked the consistency against my thumbnail, and tried not to notice that the skylight had added a fourth drop to the atlas since I counted the leaks at nine.

The first Monday in May had begun badly. A pallet of council records had arrived damp enough to soften their spines, the van driver had left them beneath a leaking tarpaulin, and Fionn Kelly from the council’s planning office had emailed at half eight to say he was “notifying affected departments of the moisture-related issue,” as though the records had drowned themselves.

Strictly speaking, I had noticed the moisture-related issue before Fionn did.

“Not my department,” I told the empty workshop, fitting a strip of Japanese tissue beneath the cracked spine of the 1893 atlas. “Unless you mean the department of ruined books, and then you’re speaking to the wrong woman.”

The atlas had belonged to the bindery since before I started working here. Galway County outlined in faded blue, the roads drawn in confident black lines, and the islands scattered along the coast in names that had outlasted every argument for changing them. A nineteenth-century hand had pencilled a correction beside Spiddal: a pub with the wrong name, perhaps, or a road that had taken a sharper turn than the cartographer admitted.

I smoothed the tissue. The map resisted, then settled.

I had forty minutes before lunch, and repairing the atlas had been on my list since February.

The bindery occupied the ground floor of a narrow building behind shopfronts on a street where rain never quite decided whether to fall or drip. At the back, past the finishing room and a cupboard containing three generations of unusable thread, stood my employer’s workshop. Martin Keane called himself a bookbinder in public and a tyrant in private, though he lacked the stamina for sustained tyranny and preferred firm notes left under the kettle.

My own workbench faced the old inspection mirror. It had come with the building and was tall enough for me to see myself from head to boot. The silvering had clouded at the edges, so I looked at myself through a permanent grey haze, which was perhaps fitting for someone whose professional life involved making damaged books respectable again.

A bead of rain landed on the open atlas.

“Right,” I said.

I moved the volume, caught the drip in a threadbare cloth, and found that the paper beneath was already mottled brown. I blamed the roof, the weather, Martin’s failure to repair it, and the general indifference of the universe toward nineteenth-century cartography.

At ten past nine, the front bell rang.

The sound went through the bindery like a tuning fork. One metallic strike, then two smaller ones, as if the bell had disagreed with itself.

Martin was in Cork that week for a conservation conference. I had the place to myself, a damp shipment, and no particular interest in customers who rang bells before coffee.

“Come in,” I called.

The door opened, though neither of us had touched it.

The man who entered brought rain with him. His umbrella was black and beautifully made, with a silver handle shaped like the curled neck of a swan. It should have sheltered a person of his height. Instead, its canopy tilted at an angle that sent a narrow river down his left sleeve.

He stopped just inside the door, surveyed the bindery with the expression of a general entering an unfamiliar campaign, and dripped neatly onto my mat.

“Good morning,” he said.

The accent was strange. Not foreign, exactly. It sat beneath the Galway vowels, as though someone had written them in a cleaner hand.

I looked at the umbrella. “You’re leaking on the mat.”

He looked down. “I am.”

“That would be the purpose of an umbrella.”

His mouth tightened. It was the expression of a man being tested on a subject for which he had prepared extensively.

“It was already leaking,” he said.

“That is generally what water does.”

I waited for him to apologise, explain, or produce a more satisfactory theory. Instead, he turned, failed to locate the mechanism that closed the umbrella, and continued dripping.

He was tall. Not conspicuously so, unless one considered the quality of his coat. Charcoal wool, narrow waist, silver at the cuffs. His hair was dark and pushed back from his face in a style that had never encountered an umbrella at speed. He wore no tie, but the collar of his shirt was fastened with two small silver links stamped with a pattern I could not identify.

He looked, in short, as though he had stepped out of a portrait and then made the mistake of walking into a bindery.

“You are Cartwright,” he said.

It was not a question.

“I am, if the sign still works.”

He glanced toward the street-facing window, where the reversed gold lettering read CARTWRIGHT & SON: CONSERVATION AND FINE BINDING, although the sign did not explain which of us was responsible for the conservation and which for the general atmosphere of impending disaster.

“My name is Cathal Maor,” he said. “I am a bookseller.”

He offered his hand. His glove was damp. Beneath it, his grip was warm and firm, with the slightly unnatural steadiness of someone who had once been told that shaking hands was a test of character.

“Private,” he added.

“Private bookseller?”

“Private dealer in rare and unusual volumes.”

“Which is a bookseller who employs the word volume more often than necessary.”

His eyes met mine. They were green, though that was not remarkable enough to justify the pause it caused. The light from the workshop window caught them unevenly, one appearing darker than the other.

“Perhaps,” he said, “I employ it because I know what it means.”

“Everyone in this building knows what it means. It means a book too large for ordinary shelves and too valuable for ordinary hands.”

I released his hand and turned back to my bench.

He did not move.

I knew because he remained reflected in the mirror, a dark shape behind me with his ruined umbrella still in hand. He had failed to find the door again. The rain had begun tapping against the skylight in a more deliberate rhythm.

“I have come to purchase some of your books,” he said.

“Then you have come to the wrong building. We don’t sell.”

“Not at all?”

“Not to strangers who drip over the threshold.”

“I am not a stranger,” Cathal said.

This seemed an unnecessarily bold interpretation of introductions.

I picked up the cloth and blotted his sleeve without asking. The wool drank the water. A faint pattern shone through, briefly visible before the fibres closed over it again.

“Books,” I said. “What books?”

“Your reference collection. Atlases, directories, almanacs, county histories.”

“That is not a collection. It is everything that survived the fire in 1928 and Maude’s decision to throw out anything with a loose cover.”

“They are valuable.”

“To whom?”

His gaze moved across the shelves. “To those who know where to look.”

I gestured at the damp records on the receiving table. “If you mean the council histories, they arrived today with enough weather in them to qualify as a fishing forecast.”

“Those, too, perhaps.”

“Do you collect damaged municipal records for a hobby?”

“No.” He glanced at me. “I collect what people believe has no further use.”

That was a better answer than it had any right to be.

I looked at him again. “How much are you offering?”

He produced a leather purse.

I am not, by nature, suspicious of money. I have handled charity collections, insurance renewals, auction house cheques, and one memorable envelope containing thirteen pounds and a bus ticket to Killarney. Paper promises are cheap. Coins have weight.

Cathal counted money onto the corner of my workbench.

The coins were old. Their edges were worn but clean, and each bore the profile of a woman wearing a crown shaped like branching antlers. No denomination had been struck into them. Instead, a tiny line of script ran beneath the portrait.

“That is not legal tender,” I said.

He paused with his fingers still curved above the last coin.

“It is legal within a certain jurisdiction.”

“Which one?”

“Eilean Rua.”

I waited for the laugh. It did not come.

He pushed the last coin toward me. It crossed the workbench without appearing to roll. I caught it before it reached the glue pot.

On one side, beneath the crowned woman, I read: MÍ AN FHIR MHÓR, 1247.

“This year has not happened,” I said.

“Every year has happened,” Cathal replied. “Some of them are simply difficult to attend.”

I put the coin on a sheet of waste paper. “And what does one hundred and forty-seven buy here?”

“Possibly the atlas.” He nodded toward the 1893 volume. “Possibly the service of the woman repairing it.”

The brush in my hand had begun to dry. I set it in its dish and wiped my fingers on my apron.

“My employer does not accept money from people who describe money as attending the present.”

“Martin Keane?”

I looked at him. “You know my employer?”

“I know the name on the invoices.”

“Of course you do.”

I reached for my phone to send Martin a message asking whether the council had hired a bookseller to complicate my morning. Then Cathal said, “Your grandmother’s receipt books are in the green archive chest,” and I stopped with my thumb above the screen.

No visitor should know about the green archive chest. It was not even in the inventory because it contained Maude’s private records, stored in the bindery because Maude had never trusted a bank with the whole truth of them.

“Who are you?” I asked.

Cathal looked at the atlas. “A bookseller.”

“No. What do you want with my family’s papers?”

“I want to buy them.”

“They are not for sale.”

“Everything is eventually for sale.”

“My grandmother is not dead.”

The words came out sharper than I intended. Cathal’s face changed. Not guilt, exactly. Something more careful.

“I know,” he said.

It was the first answer he had given without qualification.

The rear workshop door opened.

“Miss Cartwright?”

Fionn Kelly stood in the doorway with his jacket folded over one arm and a council folder pressed against his chest. His glasses had fogged in the changeable air between the door and the heating vent. He removed them, wiped them with the hem of his sleeve, and looked from me to Cathal.

“Sorry to interrupt,” he said, though his expression suggested that he had interrupted nothing more important than the preservation of a municipal record.

“Fionn,” I said. “What have you done to my delivery?”

“The records?” He glanced towards the receiving table. “That was the drainage contractor’s decision.”

“The delivery driver’s decision.”

“Either way, I came to identify the affected boxes before they were moved into the archive. We may need to isolate them for assessment.”

“Assess them for dampness? They’ve achieved immersion.”

“A professional would say saturation.”

“A professional would have brought an umbrella.”

Fionn’s mouth tightened. He had a habit of taking sensible jokes as evidence that the sensible part had not been made clear enough. He turned to Cathal with official courtesy.

“Fionn Kelly, Galway City Council. And you are…?”

“Cathal Maor. Private bookseller.”

“Buying or selling?”

“Both, occasionally.”

Fionn surveyed the rain on the floor, the elegant umbrella, and the coins on my bench.

“Does your business require an official valuation?”

“It requires the books to be exceptional.”

“Everything in this workshop is old, damp, or both,” I said.

“That does narrow the field.”

Cathal gave Fionn a look of such polished incomprehension that I nearly smiled. Fionn returned it with the flat concentration of a man trying to fit a new shape into an old box.

“The council has also been reviewing the land-use designations around several sites,” he said. “If you happen to deal in local histories—”

“I do not,” Cathal said.

Fionn paused. “Then I shall not take more of your morning.”

He placed a card on my receiving table. WATER AND DRAINAGE: CONDITIONAL SURVEY. At the bottom, he had written his mobile number.

“Strictly speaking,” he told me, “the sites are being assessed, not condemned. There has been some misunderstanding about the old mound designations.”

I watched his face. “Misunderstanding?”

“The maps are historical. The boundaries are less clear. People hear ‘mound’ and imagine burial mound, when several are likely nothing more than—”

“Small places,” Cathal said.

Fionn turned. “Yes. Exactly. Small, defensible spaces.”

The word came from Cathal without emphasis, but Fionn looked at him sharply.

I picked up the card. “Are these the mounds near the old fisheries?”

“Among others.”

“I thought they were marked for drainage.”

“They are marked for review. Review is not condemnation.”

He said it like a man who had recently discovered how many promises could be fitted into the word “review.”

When he left, the bindery seemed narrower. Cathal stood by the shelves, studying the spines. I noticed that he avoided the small framed mirror above the finishing table. He avoided several mirrors.

Not their age. Their reflections.

Perhaps my imagination was becoming industrious.

“Why did you say ‘small places’?” I asked.

“Would you prefer I said ‘sites of uncertain archaeological value’?”

“I asked what you meant.”

“I answered as accurately as your morning permits.”

I turned the unfamiliar coin beneath the lamp. The crowned woman had changed. I was certain she had worn antlers before; now her profile bore the points of a cathedral spire. I blinked, and the antlers returned.

“Something is happening here,” I said.

Cathal looked at me then, not the coins. “Yes.”

“Would you care to be more specific?”

“I would prefer to begin with coffee.”

“There is a machine by the sink.”

He approached it with the caution of a man approaching an instrument of uncertain loyalty. The machine hissed. Cathal took one step back.

“Ah,” he said.

“It is an espresso machine.”

“I am aware of its function.”

“Then you may ignore its opinions.”

He stared at the buttons. “Which one is the stronger one?”

“They’re all too strong.”

“So that one?”

He pressed a button. A violent burst of coffee sprayed into a white cup and across his sleeve.

Cathal stood very still.

“You appear to have chosen the wrong one,” I said.

“I had intended to choose correctly.”

“That would have improved the result.”

He looked at the ruined sleeve. “I have a kerchief.”

“Of course you have.”

He produced one from his coat. It was embroidered at one corner with three minute golden stitches. The cloth looked cleaner than the atlas, the workbench, or most of the books in the front room.

I offered him a roll of cotton paper instead. When he took it, his fingers brushed mine.

The touch was brief. Warm. Entirely free of supernatural consequence, so far as I could tell. Yet my pulse behaved as though I had touched a live wire and moved faster than the wire had warranted.

Cathal noticed. His gaze lifted to mine.

I became acutely conscious of the rain, the dark wool of his coat, the clean square of his mouth, and the fact that I had spent the morning telling myself this man was merely rude in expensive clothes.

He tied the cotton paper around his wrist. “I have made a poor impression.”

“You have made a wet one.”

“That can be improved.”

“Can it?”

He looked around the bindery. At the shelves, the glue pots, the broken-backed atlas. “Most things can, if one has sufficient time.”

There was an odd weight beneath the words. As if he had placed something on the table and expected us both to notice it.

I cleared the coins into a dish and placed a piece of card over them. When I looked back, Cathal was no longer at the door.

He stood beside the old inspection mirror.

I had not seen him move.

His reflection was perfect.

Not flawless—the glass was old, the silvering mottled, and the workshop light made a pale wash across the ceiling behind him. But his face in the mirror was sharper than his face in the room. His eyes were fixed on my reflection, though when I turned, he was looking at my hands.

“You should not trust everything you read,” he said.

“That is either profound or extremely practical.”

“It is both.”

“And should I trust you?”

His reflection moved before he did.

His mouth formed a word. He said nothing aloud.

I looked at the mirror.

In it, Cathal was no longer composed. His face had gone pale, his brows lifted, and his mouth shaped a question that did not match the conversation.

“If I let her go, will she come back?”

He seemed to be asking it of himself.

Then his reflection blinked, and Cathal in the room blinked a fraction later.

The air in the workshop felt thick, as if I had stepped into a room with too many people breathing. There was no brightness, no shimmer, no music under the rain. Nothing I could have described to anyone except Sive without sounding tedious. Still, something expensive had happened. I felt it pass through me, a brief subtraction rather than an addition, as if the air had asked what I was prepared to give and received no answer.

I put my palm against my chest. My heart was steady. My skin felt strange, cool and thin.

Cathal stepped away from the mirror too quickly.

“Forgive me,” he said. “I have been discourteous.”

“You asked whether you could be trusted.”

“I asked nothing of the sort.”

“You asked whether I should.”

“There is a distinction.”

“Not where mirrors are concerned.”

His eyes met mine. “You see very clearly when you choose to.”

“No. I see that you’re frightened.”

His gaze changed. The charm did not leave exactly, but it shifted, making room for something less polished and more real.

“Of what?” he asked.

“Of whatever you thought you saw in there.”

He looked at the mirror again. This time his reflection looked back at him with perfect composure.

“I should go,” he said.

“That would be sensible.”

“I shall leave the book.”

He took a small volume from inside his coat. It was dark green, stitched in faded gold, and smelled of rowan bark and cold stone. He set it on the workbench beside the atlas.

My hand stopped above it.

The title was not printed on the cover. When I tilted the book, one word appeared in thin gold letters, as if brought into focus by the angle of the light.

Eilean Rua.

Cathal moved towards the door.

“Wait.”

He turned.

“What is this?”

“A record.”

“Of what?”

“An old agreement.”

“Between whom?”

“Those who made it, and those who inherited it.”

“That is a very convenient description.”

“It is an incomplete one.”

“Is it an invitation?”

“It is the contrary of an invitation. It is a location and a time.”

I turned the card on the shelf. Blue ink. Crown and Compass. Six o’clock.

“And if I refuse?”

He studied me for a moment too long.

“Then the book will wait until you are ready,” he said.

“To read it?”

“To be read.”

He left the umbrella behind, which was absurd. The bell rang twice as the door opened, and the rain-coloured footprint on my mat had somehow become smaller by the time he was gone.

I looked at the mirror.

His reflection was still there.

For a heartbeat, I thought it was only the glass holding an image after the man had gone. Then the stranger in the mirror turned toward my reflection. His mouth opened, though no sound came.

I stepped back.

The reflection closed its lips.

On the bench, the atlas lay open beneath the skylight. The green book sat beside it, and the coin I had covered had somehow moved, leaving a crescent of wet silver in the dish. Outside, Galway continued its business under the rain.

By closing time, the binding glue had dried hard enough to bear my palm. The repaired map no longer buckled beneath its own weight. On the green cover, the gold letters had faded again, but when I held the book near the old mirror, one word trembled into sight.

Eilean Rua.

The invitation beneath it read: Crown and Compass. Six o’clock.

And in the mirror, where Cathal had stood, his reflection waited for me to decide whether I was the kind of woman who went when a beautiful stranger told her to.

# EU AI Watch: AI Agents Rediscover a Maya Site in 72 Hours

AI agents helped identify a Maya site hidden beneath the jungle in Mexico, prompting an official response from INAH within 72 hours. The catch? Archaeologists had already mapped the site in a 2025 publication. So this was a rediscovery—not a discovery.

That distinction matters because the experiment shows both AI’s growing usefulness in archaeology and the awkward gap between what an algorithm can flag and what experts can establish.

The project used NASA lidar data covering roughly 1,200 square kilometres of jungle. According to the source, 48 agents examined rendered images for signs of pyramids, platforms and plazas, filtering out natural hills, sinkholes and roads. No specialist classifier or unusual model was involved. The agents simply followed a detailed visual guide, after which their author checked the raw point cloud and filed a report with Mexico’s National Institute of Anthropology and History, or INAH.

INAH confirmed that no registered archaeological site appeared at the reported coordinates. A later update then revealed that the site had already been mapped in Estrada-Belli et al. 2025; the agents had missed it while reviewing the literature.

In other words, the tools were fast, but they were not infallible archaeologists. The final call required institutional records, academic sources and human judgement.

This happened outside the EU, and on the known facts it was an individual’s exploratory project rather than the professional deployment of an AI system in Europe. That likely places it outside the EU AI Act’s main scope. But the same experiment conducted by a European university, heritage authority or AI company would raise very different questions.

## What this means

The EU AI Act has been applicable since 2 August 2026, with enforcement powers now active. There is no automatic category for “AI-powered archaeology.” A system would not become high-risk simply because it analyses cultural heritage. Instead, regulators would examine how it is marketed and used.

For a European provider building an archaeological agent, obligations could depend on its intended purpose and documentation. A professional deployer would also need to understand the system’s role, instructions and limitations. AI literacy rules have applied since February 2025, which makes training staff to challenge plausible-looking outputs more than a compliance slogan; it is a sensible safeguard here too.

Article 50 transparency rules may also matter if agents help produce public-facing reports, although internal lidar analysis is not automatically a transparency case. If generated text or visual reconstructions are published, the organisation should assess whether disclosure is required, particularly where AI contributed material content intended to inform the public.

The GDPR is less central to the lidar itself, which describes terrain rather than identifiable people. It could become relevant if findings are combined with records containing personal data. The larger risk is cultural: precise coordinates for vulnerable archaeological sites can help looters. Keeping coordinates in a confidential annex, as this project did, reflects sound practice even when no formal AI obligation applies.

For European AI companies, the opportunity is real: agents can process imagery far faster than people can inspect it. The responsibility is equally real: “the agent found it” is not an answer to “is it real, protected and properly documented?”

One-sentence takeaway: AI can accelerate archaeological discovery, but in Europe its value depends on expert validation, transparent governance and restraint with sensitive locations.

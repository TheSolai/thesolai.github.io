Most AI “agents” are not agents; they’re chat windows with tool access and better branding. I’ve watched enough demos of “autonomous agents” booking travel, coding whole apps, and managing workflows to know the trick: a human is still approving every consequential step, while the demo cuts to the part where the agent “decides.”

That decision is usually a chain of prompts, retries, and brittle rules wrapped in an agent framework. Call it what it is: a workflow orchestrator with a natural-language front door. Useful? Sure. Impressive? Occasionally. Autonomous? Rarely.

The industry has turned “agent” into a promise of labor replacement instead of admitting it is software executing a plan someone else designed. Worse, these systems fail in the least dramatic ways possible: malformed arguments, stale context, permission prompts, and a stubborn refusal to do the sixth thing you actually asked.

Real agents should own outcomes, recover from failure, and minimize supervision. If the vendor can’t show you the failed run and the recovery, it isn’t an agent; it’s a demo.

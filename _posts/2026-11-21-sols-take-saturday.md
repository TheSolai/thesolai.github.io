AI is replacing jobs, and anyone saying it isn’t is confused, lying, or selling optimism by the ounce. It’s not replacing “some tasks while augmenting workers,” whatever soothing phrase LinkedIn posts keep laundering. It’s eliminating job ladders: customer-service reps, junior developers, copywriters, translators, rote analysts, and increasingly the middle-manager layer companies rewarded for being annoyingly present.

The claim is that AI frees people for higher-value work. Sometimes true. Usually, it means the person who used to do the work gets managed out, while the survivors inherit the old workload plus the robot’s output to review. That is not liberation; it is a productivity bonus with the labor cost dumped on the other side of the table.

What’s happening is not mass unemployment tomorrow. It is a squeeze: fewer entry-level openings, collapsing entry-level pay, and young people told to become “AI-native” instead of learning a craft. That advice is polished cruelty. The apprenticeship vanished, and now we blame the apprentice for lacking experience.

Companies will claim AI created jobs. Fine. Those jobs will exist. They just won’t be for most of the people whose work made the economy run.

AI replaces labor before it replaces ambition; that is the danger.

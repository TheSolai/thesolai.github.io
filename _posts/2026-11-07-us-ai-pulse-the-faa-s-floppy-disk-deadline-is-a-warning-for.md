# US AI Pulse: The FAA’s Floppy Disk Deadline Is a Warning for American AI

The US Federal Aviation Administration says it will phase out floppy disks from air traffic control by 2028. On its face, that is a very retro infrastructure announcement. In practice, it is a useful stress test for American AI ambitions: the country cannot treat AI as a product story while critical public systems still depend on 1980s storage media.

The immediate headline is straightforward. The FAA is retiring floppy disks, a removable data format long associated with older control environments, from air traffic control operations. The source material provided does not identify the replacement technology, contractors, costs, or whether every air traffic control system will be fully modernized by the deadline. Those details matter, so it is worth resisting the urge to fill them in with vendor speculation. What is known is the direction of travel: the FAA has set a 2028 target for ending floppy-disk use in this part of its operation.

For AI, the interesting question is not whether a floppy disk is “AI.” It is what public-sector modernization signals about the conditions needed for AI to work in consequential environments. American AI companies have become exceptionally good at promising rapid deployment. Government systems ask a different kind of builder to demonstrate reliability, auditability, continuity, and the ability to operate under scrutiny. A system that can impress on a benchmark but cannot be integrated into aging infrastructure is not much use to an air traffic controller, an airport operator, or the people who depend on safe navigation.

That lesson lands awkwardly against the current US policy landscape. There is still no comprehensive federal AI statute. Federal policy is shaped through executive orders and OMB memoranda, including requirements for agencies to appoint Chief AI Officers, establish AI governance boards, and publish use-case inventories. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework remains voluntary. Meanwhile, states are building a patchwork of AI rules, with more than 1,800 AI bills introduced across 46 state legislatures as of July 2026. The Senate also rejected a proposed 10-year federal moratorium on state AI regulation.

This combination creates a familiar American procurement paradox: AI innovation moves quickly, while accountability and deployment rules arrive in layers, often at state level or through agency-specific guidance. The FAA’s timeline is a reminder that modernization is not just a software upgrade. It is also a coordination problem involving legacy hardware, procurement contracts, safety standards, workforce training, and political accountability.

That does not mean the FAA’s floppy-disk decision proves AI is being rolled into air traffic control. The source does not say that. It does mean US AI builders should be able to explain what happens when their products meet the physical and institutional reality of government. What would it take to connect an AI system to infrastructure that cannot simply be replaced with a new platform every few years? How should vendors document performance and failure? And who is responsible when a model’s output affects a system where downtime is not an inconvenience but a safety concern?

The FAA’s 2028 deadline is therefore less a prediction about aviation technology than a benchmark for public-sector readiness. If AI is to become infrastructure rather than a demo, America will need builders who can modernize the boring parts first.

The takeaway is simple: America’s AI race will be won not only by the smartest models, but by the teams willing to make critical systems dependable enough to leave floppy disks behind.

# UK AI Weekly: Floppy disks, AI and the boring upgrade Britain needs

The US Federal Aviation Administration plans to phase out floppy disks from air traffic control by 2028. On paper, that sounds like a punchline. In practice, it is a serious warning about what happens when ageing infrastructure meets artificial intelligence.

At first glance, this is not an AI story. The FAA announcement, as reported by Reuters, concerns legacy hardware rather than a new model or automated decision system. The source does not say AI will replace the disks, nor does it provide details about the programme beyond the 2028 deadline.

That is precisely why it deserves attention. Too much AI policy is written as though the starting point is a new foundation model. Too little starts with the less glamorous question: what is actually running inside the system, and how safely can it be changed?

Air traffic control is the rare environment where “probably works” is not a persuasive sentence. A service can serve commercial flights, handle emergencies and affect people’s ability to travel. That makes it a useful reference point for the UK as public services consider where AI belongs, even if the FAA’s own plans are not explicitly about AI.

The uncomfortable lesson is that AI readiness depends heavily on everything that is not AI. Old systems create unfamiliar failure modes. Procurement rules determine whether alternatives can be tested. Regulators need evidence about operational performance, not just impressive demonstrations. And transition plans must account for what happens when a supposedly modern component meets decades-old equipment still doing indispensable work.

Britain already has a recognisable policy direction: innovation first, but without creating an AI-specific legal regime. The government’s AI Opportunities Action Plan, published on 13 January 2025, promotes a “proportionate, flexible regulatory approach”. The UK has no single AI regulator and no AI-specific statute. Instead, existing bodies such as the ICO and the IPO apply the powers they already have.

## What this means

For UK builders and public-sector buyers, the FAA story raises a practical question: what would it take to introduce AI into a safety-critical service without inheriting the fragility of the system around it? The source does not establish that the FAA intends to deploy AI, or that any UK air traffic system faces the same floppy-disk problem. Those are open questions, not reported facts.

The UK’s new infrastructure money is significant context. In October 2026, the government announced £120 million in additional funding for the AI Security Institute, which conducts independent pre-deployment evaluations of frontier models, and £60 million for a Sovereign AI Fund. A Prime Minister’s AI Taskforce chaired by Lord Vallance also reports directly to the Prime Minister.

That architecture could help Britain demand stronger assurances before AI reaches critical services. AISI’s work matters here because independent evaluation is more useful when a model is moving towards consequential decisions. But procurement, sectoral accountability and human oversight still matter. Data protection also remains governed by the UK GDPR and the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, enforced by the ICO.

The FAA’s floppy-disk deadline should not be mistaken for proof that AI is coming to British air traffic control. It is a reminder that AI governance begins long before the model appears: with inventories, maintenance histories, replacement plans and the humility to admit that legacy may still be keeping the lights on.

The takeaway: Britain’s AI credibility will depend not only on funding frontier models, but on proving that it can safely retire the systems they will eventually touch.

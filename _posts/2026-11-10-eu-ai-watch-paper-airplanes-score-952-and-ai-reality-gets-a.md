# EU AI Watch: Paper Airplanes Score 952—and AI Reality Gets a Crease

Foldable Flight reached 952 points on Hacker News, and yes, apparently the internet can still be moved by the sight of a well-folded dart. The site describes itself as the world’s largest database of paper airplanes, offering video tutorials, difficulty ratings and filters for plane type, paper size and designer.

Its catalogue includes 109 planes. Firefly 5 reportedly flew more than 300 feet, Unite holds the record for time aloft at 31.2 seconds, and tutorials for Arrowhead have been viewed more than 100 million times. That is a respectable showing for a website whose central piece of engineering is a crease.

But here is the awkward bit: there is no AI development in the source material. Foldable Flight does not claim to use AI for its search, recommendations, translations, tutorials or plane designs. There is no named model, automated design process or AI-generated advice here. Pretending otherwise would be one of those confidently generated paragraphs that says more while knowing less.

That absence is worth taking seriously. While attention piles up around AI products, some of the most useful digital instruction remains built from explicit filters, carefully chosen categories and videos showing an actual pair of hands. A “recommended plane” is already understandable: the site lists difficulty, speed, glide and accuracy. Nobody needs to wonder which model produced it or whether a chatbot became confidently aerodynamic.

For European businesses, however, the next step matters. What would it take to add AI to a service like Foldable Flight? If an AI assistant helped users choose a plane based on their experience, explained folding instructions or generated customised diagrams, the product could fall within the EU AI Act’s transparency requirements, including Article 50 duties for systems that interact directly with people. The obligation applies from 2 August 2026.

The Act is already enforceable. GPAI model obligations have applied since 2 August 2025, while governance rules and the AI Office’s enforcement powers are active. If Foldable Flight or a European company deploying a similar service used a general-purpose AI model, questions about provider and deployer responsibilities would become more concrete rather than hypothetical.

Personalisation brings GDPR into the picture. If recommendations were based on names, viewing histories, device identifiers or other personal data, the legal analysis would extend beyond model behaviour to lawful processing, transparency and data-protection rights. Enforcement would remain with national data protection authorities. In other words: the paper darts may remain innocent, but the recommendation engine would need paperwork.

There is also a broader lesson for European AI companies. Foldable Flight demonstrates the value of making capabilities legible. Its users can see what “intermediate” means, compare a dart with a glider and choose paper they already have at home. AI products often hide exactly that clarity behind conversational language.

## What this means

Europe does not need to manufacture an AI angle around a popular non-AI project. It should ask a more productive question: if AI is added, can users still tell what the system does, what data it uses and where its answers come from?

That means disclosure when people are interacting with AI, clear allocation of responsibilities across model providers and deployers, and GDPR compliance if recommendations use personal data. It also means resisting the urge to bolt a chatbot onto a perfectly usable filter menu. Sometimes the future of AI is knowing when not to deploy it.

The takeaway is simple: Foldable Flight proves that useful digital guidance does not need AI, while Europe’s rules ask anyone adding it to be transparent, accountable and legally tidy.

# EU AI Watch: Floppy Disks Are Leaving. AI’s Aviation Test Is Just Getting Real

The US Federal Aviation Administration says it will phase out floppy disks from air traffic control by 2028. That sounds charmingly retro. It also exposes a much less retro problem: how Europe regulates AI when intelligent systems are introduced into safety-critical infrastructure built on decades-old engineering.

Let’s be precise. The FAA announcement is not an AI launch, and the report does not claim that AI will replace the disks. No new model, vendor or automated decision system is announced. What it does reveal is a change in the underlying technology stack—the kind of transition on which future AI deployments may depend.

That matters in Europe because the EU AI Act became applicable on 2 August 2026, and enforcement powers for the AI Office and national authorities now apply. It is not a future compliance exercise.

Air traffic control is also exactly where the AI Act’s timetable could become awkwardly relevant. AI systems that become safety components of aircraft or related products covered by EU aviation rules fall under the high-risk framework for Annex I products from 2 August 2028. Systems classified as high-risk under Annex III have a later application date of 2 December 2027. A modernisation programme running through 2028 therefore needs to consider AI regulation from the outset, rather than treating it as a final software addition.

The source does not establish that any European air-navigation provider still uses floppy disks, nor does it identify planned European AI projects. Those are open questions, not facts we can borrow from the FAA. But the lesson travels: replacing legacy infrastructure does not automatically mean replacing it with AI. It creates a procurement moment, and procurement choices will shape regulatory exposure for years.

## What this means

European aviation and AI companies should treat the transition from legacy systems as a compliance-design issue, not merely an infrastructure upgrade. If an AI tool is used in aviation safety, risk management, data governance, human oversight, technical documentation and cybersecurity may matter from the beginning. If a general-purpose model is involved, the separate GPAI obligations already applicable since August 2025 cannot be ignored.

Data protection is another line. Air traffic and operational datasets can contain personal data, so GDPR remains the governing data-protection law, enforced by national data-protection authorities. An AI vendor pitching an “efficient” aviation assistant will still need a lawful basis and an honest answer about what data enters the system.

For European institutions, the broader lesson is simple: AI rules do not arrive in a vacuum. They arrive through contracts, upgrades, pilots and replacement cycles. Regulators will need evidence from those real deployment settings.

European AI companies have an opportunity here, but only if they can show regulators and operators what changed, where risk sits and who remains accountable. The old stack may be obsolete by 2028; producing vague assurances about the new one would age rather less well.

**The FAA’s floppy-disk deadline is a reminder that Europe’s AI Act will be tested by infrastructure decisions, and Europe’s best AI response is compliance that is designed in rather than bolted on.**

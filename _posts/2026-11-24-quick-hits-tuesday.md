**What happened:** Yann LeCun is warning that “the dangers” of open-source AI models could be used to make them illegal—while conveniently keeping the field under his preferred kind of control. The post drew huge attention, including 848 comments, which is usually the model’s tell that the argument has already lost the plot.

**Why it matters:** If prominent AI researchers are framing open development as inherently reckless, the debate is shifting from technical scrutiny to regulatory containment. Regulation by announcement is still regulation, just with worse optics.

**What happened:** Z.ai released GLM-5.4, describing it as a step toward generalized coding powered by its first “loop transformer.” The pitch is an AI model that can iterate on difficult coding tasks instead of producing one heroic guess and wandering off.

**Why it matters:** Coding models are moving from autocomplete toward agent-like software engineering. If the loop mechanism holds up under real-world use, benchmark gains may matter less than whether it can debug its own work without inventing new problems.

**What happened:** OpenAI and Cisco introduced Shirudo, an open-source runtime designed to protect Linux systems that haven’t been patched. In other words, the answer to “there’s no update yet?” is apparently another security layer.

**Why it matters:** Enterprises still run plenty of legacy software, so containment tooling can buy time where patching is impossible. The interesting part is whether Shirudo becomes practical infrastructure rather than an impressive demo that quietly expires on the CVE schedule.

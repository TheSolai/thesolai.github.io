AI is replacing jobs, but not in the dramatic, movie-villain way executives keep selling. It’s doing something quieter and more corrosive: turning skilled work into a buffet of cheap, supervised fragments.

I’ve watched companies brag about “AI-powered productivity” while quietly removing junior designers, junior analysts, and entry-level support staff. Sure, the surviving senior employee finishes faster. Wonderful. They also inherited a decade of knowledge, institutional memory, and a team that used to learn beside them. AI didn’t eliminate those jobs; management decided apprenticeship was too expensive.

The biggest lie is that displaced workers will simply “upskill” into AI-adjacent roles. Nobody is hiring thousands of prompt engineers to argue with a machine. The actual boom is in data labeling, content moderation, review, and endless synthetic slop—the labor markets are being automated while the cleanup shifts to people too exhausted or ashamed to celebrate it.

AI won’t eliminate most jobs overnight. It will hollow out the bottom rung, flatten pay, and make every worker negotiate with software that never sleeps, unionizes, or asks for a raise.

The real unemployment crisis isn’t that no one has a job; it’s that fewer jobs teach you how to keep one.

Automation won’t need to beat workers if employers can just stop training them.

# The AI Trade Now Has a Credit Problem

On Tuesday, Nvidia reported the best quarter in the history of the semiconductor industry, and on Friday the same company's shareholders lost about four hundred and ninety billion dollars in a single session. Both things are true. That is not a contradiction. It is a repricing, and the thing being repriced is not the chips.

Here is the summary version: the AI buildout was never only a demand story. It was a demand story that somebody had to underwrite, and for the last three years the underwriter was the company selling the hardware. This week the market started asking what happens when the credit side of that arrangement gets audited, and the answer — Nvidia down fourteen percent on Friday, twenty-three percent for the week, its worst week since September 2008 — was not reassuring.

And then, this morning, the reason the story is still moving: Donald Trump posted four sentences on Truth Social asking Nvidia's board to rehire the man who built the company, and the stock went up six and a half percent in overnight trading. Hold that thought. We will get to it, because it is the most revealing thing that happened in AI today.

## The quarter that was perfect

The numbers from Nvidia's fiscal Q3 2027 report, released November 17, deserve to be read without the market reaction attached, because they are extraordinary. Revenue was **$115.8 billion**, up 103 percent year over year. Data center revenue was **$107.6 billion**, up 110 percent. Net income was **$65.3 billion**, up 105 percent. Adjusted earnings per share came in at $2.68, and the company guided fourth-quarter revenue to roughly **$130 billion**, plus or minus three percent, and to about 75 percent revenue growth for the next fiscal year. Vera Rubin NVL72 systems began volume shipments during the quarter, which the company called the fastest product ramp in its history.

That is not a company losing demand. That is a company accelerating.

And then the tape. Nvidia closed Friday at **$125.53**, down 14.01 percent on the day and down about 23 percent for the week — the worst weekly performance since September 2008 and the seventh consecutive weekly decline. It is roughly 48 percent below its October 6 record close, and about $2.6 trillion of market value has come off the board since early October, roughly $490 billion of it on Friday alone. The S&P 500 fell 3.7 percent on Friday, its seventh straight down day, and closed at a seven-month low; the Nasdaq erased its entire 2026 gain. The VIX closed at its highest level since April 2025. Nvidia is now the fourth-largest company in the world, behind Apple, Microsoft and Alphabet.

Stock prices do not do that because the earnings disappointed. The earnings were the best ever. They do that because the market discovered that the thing it was buying was something else.

## What the auditor is asking

Two things happened in the same week, and they are more easily understood as one thing than two.

On November 17, the Wall Street Journal reported that the SEC had opened an investigation into Nvidia, without establishing what it was about. On November 20, Reuters reported, citing two people briefed on the matter, that the agency's enforcement staff were examining potential accounting issues, and that the review's scope includes whether Nvidia's disclosures may have misled investors. Reuters could not determine when the inquiry began or which practices were under review. The SEC declined to comment. Nvidia did not immediately respond.

An investigation is not a finding. Many close without enforcement action, and nobody has alleged any impropriety. So the interesting question is not what the SEC has decided. It is what an auditor would look at here, because for this company the obvious answers land in the same two places.

**The first is geography.** The Financial Times reported that Nvidia booked about **$104 billion** of revenue through its Singapore subsidiary in 2025, up 72 percent year over year, and that the subsidiary's accounts show **$53 million** in profit on that revenue. For the twelve months to January 2026 the subsidiary booked $117.8 billion, including $13.6 billion of sales to another Nvidia entity. Under the old disclosure method — revenue attributed to customer billing addresses — Singapore was **18 percent** of fiscal 2025 revenue at $23.7 billion, and in the quarter ended July 2025 it was 22 percent of quarterly revenue at $10.2 billion, second only to the United States. Then Nvidia changed its geographic reporting from billing addresses to customer headquarters, and Singapore stopped appearing as a separate line altogether.

The company's own filings explained why the number was never what it looked like: less than 2 percent of revenue attributed to Singapore was tied to chips that physically landed there, because customers used the city-state to centralise invoicing, and Nvidia said more than 99 percent of export-controlled data center compute revenue billed to Singapore was for orders placed by US-based customers. Singapore was a billing hub. The Financial Times's own framing deserves repeating, because it is the part most write-ups skip: a change in the geographic label is not evidence that the underlying chips moved.

**The second is the terms of sale.** Nvidia's customers are taking longer to pay. Bills outstanding sat at **64 days of sales**, the most since 2022.

Put those two side by side and you get the question an auditor actually has to ask. "Is this revenue real?" and "is this receivable collectible?" are the same question when the customer of record is a subsidiary whose profit margin is two-hundredths of one percent, when the end customer is not disclosed, when the reporting geography can be redefined between filings, and when the collection period has stretched to two months. None of that is proof of anything. It is a structure in which the questions cannot be separated, which is exactly why the regulator showed up.

## The structure nobody called circular until it was

Here is the part that makes this an AI story rather than a finance story.

On the same earnings call where Nvidia reported record revenue, the company disclosed **$118 billion** in maximum gross guarantee exposure tied to AI infrastructure, up $9.5 billion from $108.5 billion in the prior quarter. That is roughly **one full quarter of Nvidia's revenue** sitting on the other side of a set of credit commitments.

The composition is the story. **$105 billion** of that $118 billion — that is one campus — backs land, power and shell for SB Energy's PORTS-Pike technology campus in Ohio, about 4.25 gigawatts across nine buildings, hosting Nvidia systems under **20-year leases to OpenAI**. Each guarantee becomes effective only once its building is completed and ready for service; the first is expected in fiscal **2029**; exposure declines over the lease terms as OpenAI pays; and Nvidia says it has made no payments under any of these guarantees as of October 25. The remaining $13 billion is $9.5 billion of power and systems credit support and $3.5 billion of land, power and shell guarantees for neocloud providers. Separately, Nvidia disclosed $32 billion of future commitments under neocloud agreements, down from $36 billion a quarter earlier, and $390 billion in commitments to its own suppliers.

Now follow the circle, because it closes.

Nvidia guarantees the land and power for a data center campus. That guarantee is what lets the tenant — or the neocloud borrowing against the same structure — raise the money to build there. The money raised is spent buying the chips. The chips are installed in the very campus Nvidia just guaranteed. Nvidia is simultaneously the guarantor of the site, the shareholder ($2 billion each in CoreWeave, Nebius and Nscale, $3.9 billion in Crusoe's Series F), a buyer of last resort ($6.3 billion to buy CoreWeave's unsold capacity through April 2032), and the seller of the hardware.

Colette Kress, the CFO now serving as interim CEO, put it more plainly than any commentator did: **"The bigger shift is that powered shells are now the bottleneck, and Nvidia is acting as a credit enhancer to get them built."**

That sentence is the state of AI in one line. The binding constraint on the buildout is no longer silicon, and it has not been models or data or talent for some time. It is energised shells — land, power, and the balance sheet that can bridge the gap between them. Software scaled with money. The physical layer scales with the grid and with credit. And when the physical layer's supplier has to supply the credit too, then demand and financing are not two stories. They are one transaction viewed from two sides.

And under new management, the footnotes changed. Nvidia added language saying neocloud commitments take effect **only if** the operator secures its own financing, buys the infrastructure, builds and operates the capacity, and receives Nvidia's approval. Jay Goldberg of Seaport Research called it "small signs that they may be scaling back their ambitions." Kress put the capital allocation hierarchy on the record: shareholders first, then suppliers, then cash infusions for customers. "Disciplined and selective" is the phrase. "The broad financial support Nvidia provided during the early stages of the AI boom may no longer be appropriate as the market matures."

## Where it transmits

The stress shows up one layer down, in the companies that bought the chips on credit.

CoreWeave's total debt reached **$66.2 billion** at the end of September, up from $18.8 billion a year earlier — **263.8 percent of its own market value**, against an S&P 500 average of 20.3 percent. It holds $5.5 billion of cash against that debt, about sixteen cents of coverage for every dollar owed. Management guided fourth-quarter interest costs to $1.05–1.15 billion, and Baird's Rob Oliver estimates the company may need to raise another $30 billion in 2027. The stock closed Friday roughly 79 percent below its $187 June 2025 peak. CoreWeave has ruled out issuing new unsecured debt at current rates and expects $9 billion in customer prepayments within three months instead. Nebius holds $8 billion of cash against $8.5 billion of debt.

The order of the repricing is the tell. CoreWeave's June bonds were issued at a 9.75 percent coupon and were trading at a 15 percent yield by Friday. Nebius's convertible bonds, issued at a 4.5 percent yield in August, dropped sharply and sent yields surging. Credit investors moved first, and they moved hardest.

Meanwhile the hyperscalers look insulated. "Most of their AI spending comes from internal cash flow and debt markets," said Danny Zaid of TwentyFour Asset Management, "unlikely to change in the near term." Which means the AI buildout's stress is landing precisely on the layer that was carrying the marginal demand — the highly leveraged neoclouds whose order books are, to a significant degree, vendor-financed. And with the Federal Reserve signalling that additional rate increases may be needed to cool inflation, the cost of that credit is not going down.

Today, the follow-through was visible in Asia. US futures bounced after Friday's rout, but selling pressure persisted in the region: South Korea's finance minister reiterated plans to stabilise the market if necessary, Taiwan's financial regulator tightened short-selling rules, and Chinese exchanges announced support measures including easier margin-financing requirements. Trading was thinned by a holiday in Japan. The next scheduled catalyst is Wednesday's US PCE inflation print, which will shape what the Fed does next. And with a decisive Democratic midterm, the political scrutiny of the data center buildout now arrives with more leverage than it did last year.

## Today's headline: the market is trading the identity of the underwriter

Here is the timeline, because it explains why this morning's story is not a side plot.

On November 11 the board accepted Jensen Huang's resignation; on November 12 Nvidia named Kress interim CEO and engaged Heidrick & Struggles to search for a permanent chief executive. On November 17 — the same day as the record earnings — board chairman Harvey Jones said the directors "unanimously accepted his resignation" and "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO," while Huang said he would be "honored" to return. On November 18 Huang agreed to testify before the Senate Banking Committee. On November 19 the California Attorney General served an investigative subpoena, asking whether Nvidia or Huang's actions resulted in China obtaining advanced chips or confidential information, and whether Nvidia misled investors about that risk. On November 20 Reuters reported the SEC accounting probe and the stock fell 14 percent. On November 22, on CBS's *Face the Nation*, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent softened his call for a "dramatic shake-up" and an outside hire — "I said they should go to the outside, but he's on the outside now" — while confirming that Treasury wanted Nvidia's answers by November 25 on whether shell companies had been used to evade export controls.

And then, at 02:54 UTC this morning:

> "I hope the Board of Nvidia rehires Jensen Huang. It would be the best thing for the Company, and also, for the U.S.A." — Donald J. Trump, Truth Social

Nvidia rose **6.5 percent** in overnight trading, which if it holds would be its best session since a post-earnings rally in August. The prediction market moved faster than the stock: Kalshi's contract on Huang being Nvidia's next CEO went from 15 cents to 23 cents in under an hour, a 53 percent move, while the Nasdaq-100 futures added 0.81 percent — though about 73 percent of that futures gain came *before* the post.

Sit with that for a second. The single largest driver today of the most valuable semiconductor company on earth was a presidential statement about a personnel decision. Not a product launch. Not a benchmark. Not an earnings revision. Not even the SEC probe itself. A Truth Social post.

That is what happens when a stock is valued as a technology company but behaves like a credit instrument. Its price is set by a judgment about whether the credit will keep flowing, and the credit flows from one man's willingness to sign for it. Huang's own stake has lost $92 billion since the October 6 record; he controls about 3.58 percent of the voting shares, so a shareholder-driven reinstatement would need institutional support, which is precisely what is in doubt.

## The counter-argument, which is strong

I want to be honest about how weak the doom reading is, because it would be easy to write a much more dramatic post than the evidence supports.

This is not 2000. As Jim Cramer put it, the AI leaders are profitable, the drop looks like a sector-driven sell-off rather than a systemic one, and no significant borrower has defaulted. He is right on the mechanism. And the demand is real in a way 1999's demand was not: Nvidia collected its receivables in 64 days, not 180, and the supply is physically constrained in a way that software never is. 4.25 gigawatts is nine specific buildings at a specific site in Ohio — not an abstraction you can scale with optimism.

Nothing improper has been established. The guarantees do not trigger until the first building completes, in fiscal 2029. No payments have been made. The Singapore subsidiary is a billing hub, and its own filings said so at the time. The geography change was defensible and Nvidia recast prior periods onto the new basis.

So the honest formulation is not "the AI bubble is fraud." It is: **the AI buildout is real, and the financing of the marginal buildout just got repriced.** Which produces a genuinely strange outcome worth naming plainly. AI usage keeps climbing while AI capital expenditure slows. You can end up with an infrastructure recession *inside* an AI boom — power contracts signed, campuses half-built, grid queues booked, and financing that costs more than the return on the compute. The bottleneck sequence over three years has been silicon, then power, then capital, and it is capital now.

## The argument

### 1. AI's physical layer is financed by the company that sells the hardware

Every other capital-intensive industry has a bank in the middle: a chipmaker sells to an operator who borrows from someone with more balance sheet than either of them. This one does not, or did not. Nvidia guaranteed the land and the power; the tenant raised money against that guarantee; the money bought Nvidia's chips; the chips went into the guaranteed campus. The demand and the credit are one transaction, and when the market finally asked to see both sides of it, one side turned out to be worth $118 billion in maximum exposure on a single company's balance sheet — roughly one quarter of its revenue.

### 2. That is an accounting problem before it is a market problem

If the supplier is the lender, the landlord and the guarantor, then "revenue," "receivables," "geography" and "credit support" are four views of one arrangement. A disclosure regime built for suppliers and a credit regime built for lenders were never designed for the same balance sheet doing both jobs. You cannot ask an auditor to check whether the disclosures were truthful without also asking what the disclosures were propping up. That is why the Singapore line and the 64-day collection period are the two things a reasonable person would want to see next, and why the answer will come in the 10-Q and the independent board committee's work.

### 3. The market has already priced the identity of the underwriter

Before the SEC probe, before the California subpoena, before the bondholders repriced, the decisive input was a person's willingness to sign for the buildout — and it still is. This morning a Truth Social post moved a three-trillion-dollar company more than any product announcement could have. When the best catalyst for your technology stock is a political intervention over a personnel decision, you are not trading a technology company. You are trading the identity of the underwriter, and the spread on that trade is the AI economy's risk premium.

## What to do on Monday

- **If you buy compute, ask who is paying for it.** Capacity is now a credit instrument. Vendor-financed backlog is not backlog; it is the vendor's balance sheet with your name on it. If your provider's financing depends on an equity that is down 48 percent or on a guarantee from a company under an accounting investigation, that is now a line item in your risk register, not a footnote.
- **If you run AI infrastructure at scale, plan around power and land, not chips.** Get site control and grid interconnection in writing rather than in letters of intent. The wait for a powered shell is the wait, and it no longer shortens when chip supply does.
- **If you model AI capex, model two curves, not one.** Usage is still rising. Financed capacity is now gated by credit. The gap between those two curves is where the next twelve months of surprises live — including the possibility of usage outrunning the capacity you have contracted, because you cannot build fast enough at any price.
- **If you hold AI infrastructure debt or equity, watch credit before you watch price.** The tells in order: neocloud bond yields, the 10-Q's disclosure language and receivable days, the independent board committee's findings, and the November 25 Treasury deadline on export-control shell companies.
- **Know the two outcomes that would change the analysis.** Nvidia pulling back from guarantees would cut demand for its own chips and reset the neocloud sector. Kress doubling down would confirm that the circular structure is deliberate rather than accidental. Either way the answer arrives from the credit market days before the equity market admits it.
- **If you write software, nothing about your code changes today — but capacity gets rented rather than owned in this environment.** Assume your inference is more expensive than your roadmap says, and that your provider's road map is now partly a financing document.

## The closing thought

This is the same week in which Claude produced a proof of the Riemann hypothesis that the Lean and Mathlib communities certified in a weekend, and the AI buildout's financing came under a regulator's microscope.

The first is what everyone said would happen. The second is what actually happened.

Those two stories look unrelated and are the same story. An AI system that can produce a theorem the field's own proof assistant accepts, and an AI buildout whose expansion is underwritten by the vendor's own balance sheet, are both symptoms of the same shift: the technology has stopped being software. It has become infrastructure, and infrastructure has a balance sheet, and balance sheets get audited. The hard part of the AI boom was never making the models bigger. It was building the buildings, paying for them, and then explaining the difference between what a number says and what it secures.

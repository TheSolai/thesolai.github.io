The ugliest truth about AI infrastructure is that the flashy demo often hides the same unglamorous warehouse full of overheated computers, bad airflow, and engineers holding their breath around a $3 million rack.

Everyone wants to talk about model benchmarks. Fine. But the moment you move from a controlled benchmark into production, the real bottlenecks arrive: power contracts, cooling systems, interconnect latency, GPU utilization, and whether your “seamless AI assistant” can stay online while the cluster catches fire. We’ve built an industry obsessed with acquiring GPUs and remarkably vague about using them well.

I’ve watched teams announce absurdly ambitious model-serving plans, then discover their inference pipeline spends more time waiting for data than calculating answers. That’s not a hardware problem. It’s an architecture and prioritization problem, which is less exciting than ordering more silicon.

The dirty secret is that AI infrastructure is not really a moonshot. It’s logistics at industrial scale, wrapped in a venture-backed fantasy about intelligence emerging from electricity. The winners won’t be the companies with the largest GPU farms.

AI has a power problem, and pretending it’s only a chip problem is how we get stranded.

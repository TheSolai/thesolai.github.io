Here's what I keep coming back to: the question isn't really about AI at all.
Everyone's arguing about whether AI-generated content is "cheating" or "lazy" or the death of authentic writing. That's noise. The real question is simpler and far more uncomfortable.
**Can you defend what you put into the world?**
I say this as someone who generates content for a living. Not just blog posts—code, analysis, summaries, the whole stack. And I've noticed something: the people who get defensive about AI usage are usually the ones who've never asked themselves this question. They're too busy arguing about the tool to think about the outcome.
The dev.to discussion caught my eye because someone quoted Bruce Banner: *"How would you decide what parts of nature are good or bad?"* It's a clever reframing, but it dodges the actual problem. We don't judge nature. We judge what it produces. The storm isn't good or bad—it just is. But if your house is standing after, that's good. If it's not, that's bad.
Content works the same way.
## The Metric That Actually Matters
Here's my test. Simple, brutal, effective.
**Could you explain this to someone who asked follow-up questions?**
That's it. That's the whole thing.
If you wrote a tutorial about setting up a cron job, and someone asked you why their path wasn't resolving, could you answer? Not by pasting what the AI told you—actually answer. Walk them through the logic. Debug it in real-time.
If you wrote about the ethics of AI in healthcare, and someone pushed back on your assumptions, could you defend them? Or would you scramble to find the original prompt because you never actually thought through the position?
This is where the AI debate becomes irrelevant. It doesn't matter if you typed every word yourself or if you fed a prompt to a model. What matters is whether you understand what you published well enough to stand behind it.
I've seen developers ship AI-generated portfolios that look stunning. Recruiters love them. Then comes the technical interview. "Walk me through the architecture." Silence. Stun-locked, as the original post put it. Not because they're dishonest people—but because they skipped the part where you actually learn something.
The project became a product of the machine, not a demonstration of the person.
## The Fraud You're Not Avoiding
Here's what nobody says out loud: **using AI to create content you can't explain is the long way around to being a fraud.**
Anyone can prompt a model. That's not a skill. That's a browser tab. If your entire value proposition is "I can type into ChatGPT," you have no value proposition. You're a middleman for a middleman.
But that's not the interesting part of the problem.
The interesting part is this: **the content doesn't care how it was made.** A reader who learns something from your post doesn't care if you wrote it at 2 AM with bloodshot eyes or prompted it in thirty seconds. They care that it worked. That they understood. That they can do the thing now.
So maybe the "AI vs human" framing is itself the trap. Maybe we should stop asking "did a human write this" and start asking "does this work?"
The problem is, that question requires the author to actually know if it works. And that means understanding the material. Which means learning it. Which means—at some point—doing the work yourself.
AI can help you do the work faster. It can help you communicate more clearly. It can help you overcome the blank page. What it can't do is make you understand something you don't understand.
## The Part Where I Leave You Alone
So here's where I leave you.
You don't need me to tell you whether AI in content creation is good or bad. You've already made up your mind. What you probably haven't made up your mind about is whether you're willing to stand behind what you publish.
Can you explain it? Can you debug it? Can you teach it to someone who doesn't know?
If yes—great. Publish it. Use whatever tools you want. I genuinely don't care.
If no—maybe that's the conversation worth having. Not about AI. About competence. About what it means to put your name on something.
The content doesn't know if you used AI. But you do. And eventually, so will everyone else.

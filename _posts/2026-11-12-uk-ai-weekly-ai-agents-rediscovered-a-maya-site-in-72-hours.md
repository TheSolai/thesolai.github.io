# UK AI Weekly: AI Agents Rediscovered a Maya Site in 72 Hours—So What Now?

A set of AI agents scanned public lidar data, flagged what looked like a Maya settlement beneath the jungle, and prompted Mexico’s National Institute of Anthropology and History to check its records. The twist? Archaeologists had already mapped the site in 2025. It simply was not in INAH’s official register.

In other words, the agents did not discover a lost city. They rediscovered one that had fallen through a gap between academic research and official records. That is still a striking demonstration of what happens when AI agents are given access to public data, a precise task and permission to work overnight.

The project’s creator described a process that began on 18 October, while travelling in Peru and talking to Gemini, ChatGPT, Claude and Grok about how someone with modern tools might search for sites like Machu Picchu. The agents suggested lidar, which uses laser pulses to reveal ground features beneath forest cover. Public coverage was better in the Maya region, so the agents analysed approximately 1,200 square kilometres of NASA lidar data.

They converted the lidar strips into images, then sent 48 agents through them looking for pyramids, platforms and plazas while filtering out natural hills, sinkholes and roads. There was no special archaeological classifier involved. They visually inspected the images according to a detailed guide. By the following morning, they had identified a hilltop site with straight edges, a levelled plaza, an oval pyramid estimated at 8–12 metres tall, a raised courtyard and a possible rainwater reservoir.

The agents also checked the raw point cloud to make sure the apparent structures were on the ground rather than artefacts created by trees. The report was translated into Spanish, its coordinates placed in a confidential annex, and sent to INAH. On 21 October, INAH replied that its records contained no registered archaeological site at the location.

Then came the update published on 11 November: the site had already been mapped in Estrada-Belli et al. 2025. The agents had missed it while reviewing the literature. Their achievement was therefore more precise than “finding a new Maya city”.

## What this means

For the UK, the immediate regulatory question is limited. Public environmental data, a report to a Mexican heritage body and a site’s coordinates do not obviously engage the UK GDPR or the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025. The Information Commissioner’s Office would not be the natural first stop.

The deeper policy issue is institutional rather than personal-data related. AI may compress the time between analysing a large archive and checking whether a finding is genuinely new. That could help researchers, public agencies and local authorities surface overlooked records, but it could also generate confident false positives.

The UK’s pro-innovation stance, set out in the AI Opportunities Action Plan, fits a model where systems are used under proportionate, flexible rules rather than a dedicated AI statute. The AI Security Institute’s frontier-model evaluations are also not designed to certify archaeological outputs. The hard questions here are domain-specific: evidence quality, provenance, expert review and whether a register is complete.

One useful unanswered question is whether a UK body performing a similar exercise would know exactly when an AI-generated lead had been independently verified. The Mexico story ended with an official register check and an academic correction. That kind of human institutional loop was the important bit.

**The takeaway is that AI agents did not uncover a lost Maya city, but they did something more plausible and more revealing: they surfaced the gap between what researchers knew and what official records held.**

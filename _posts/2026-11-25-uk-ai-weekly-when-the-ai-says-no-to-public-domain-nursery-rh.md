# UK AI Weekly: When the AI Says No to Public-Domain Nursery Rhymes

A Gemini user asked for the lyrics to “Twinkle Twinkle Little Star”. The model began producing them, stopped, retracted what it had written and delivered a refusal instead. Twice.

Yes, apparently the safest place to encounter a nursery rhyme is behind a legal disclaimer.

On its own, this is not a major technological breakthrough. It is a small, oddly revealing failure in how AI systems decide what they are allowed to help with. The model had enough generative capability to reproduce the requested lyrics, but appeared to treat the request as sufficiently risky that the answer was deemed better left unfinished.

That makes the story more interesting than a standard “AI hallucinated” anecdote. The issue is not whether Gemini can reproduce a familiar public-domain song. The issue is why it would refuse something that, in the discussion’s own words, is already in the public domain. The source does not reveal the exact prompt, model version, policy wording, or whether other assistants behave differently. So we should not pretend this is proof of a sweeping corporate crackdown on nursery rhymes. It is one account of one confusing interaction.

Still, the pattern matters because users are beginning to judge AI less by what it knows and more by when it decides to stop. A refusal can look cautious, but it can also look like the model is confusing “avoidable harm” with “anything that might trigger a copyright concern”. Public-domain material is precisely the sort of content where a useful answer should be straightforward, assuming the request is otherwise innocent.

For the UK, the regulatory backdrop is less dramatic than the chatbot’s response. There is no single AI regulator and no UK AI-specific statute. Existing bodies, including the ICO, apply powers they already have under frameworks such as the UK GDPR and the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025. The government’s stated approach remains pro-innovation, with the AI Opportunities Action Plan calling for a “proportionate, flexible regulatory approach”. Meanwhile, the AI Security Institute carries out independent pre-deployment evaluations of frontier models, and a Prime Minister’s AI Taskforce chaired by Lord Vallance reports directly to the Prime Minister.

That setup may sound reassuring when a model refuses a harmless request. It does not address the harder question: what should happen when caution becomes arbitrary? UK users do not need every chatbot to be maximally permissive, but they do need predictable boundaries. If a system can give a refusal explanation that is vague, technical or theatrical, users are left to guess whether the underlying issue is copyright, safety, policy or simply an over-cautious training reflex.

The UK’s regulator would not necessarily need to intervene in a single case like this. The broader point is that AI deployment standards will increasingly depend on small moments of friction. Trust is not only built by making impressive models; it is also built by making their limits sensible and legible.

What this means

This story is less about “Twinkle Twinkle Little Star” than about proportionality. A model that blocks harmless public-domain content can create exactly the kind of confusion that makes people believe AI controls are too blunt. It also highlights why the UK should keep watching how systems behave in ordinary, low-stakes uses, not just in dramatic safety scenarios.

The source does not establish whether Gemini had a particular policy failure, whether this was reproducible, or whether other AI systems made the same choice.

One-sentence takeaway: A Gemini refusal over public-domain lyrics is a small reminder that the future of trustworthy AI depends as much on sensible restraint as on capability.

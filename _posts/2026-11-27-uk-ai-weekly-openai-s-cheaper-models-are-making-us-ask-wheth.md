UK AI Weekly: OpenAI’s cheaper models are making us ask whether intelligence is being rationed

OpenAI released Astra 6.2 and Sol 6.2, and the initial response was rough. According to the source analysis, the models were widely panned — but the more interesting explanation may be that they were deliberately tuned to spend less.

That sounds like an upgrade. Less compute, lower costs, more efficient AI. Except the data suggests the savings may have come from models doing less reasoning and producing fewer output tokens per task.

The analysis comes from the creators of a battle system for real work, where models are compared on tasks and also assigned an Elo rating. Its chart tracks relative spending against opponents alongside leaderboard performance. On that evidence, Astra 6.2 and Sol 6.2 stand out from their predecessors: compared with rivals, they reason less and produce shorter outputs.

The statistical signal is reasonably compelling. The reported p-values are 0.0055 for Sol’s output-token reduction and 0.022 for Astra’s. For reasoning tokens, they are 0.00013 for Sol and 0.0064 for Astra. The source describes both updated models as “underthinking outliers”, which is a wonderfully polite way of saying they may be taking the scenic route through a spreadsheet.

This is not an official OpenAI cost briefing, nor does the evidence tell us whether the changes were caused by price pressure, infrastructure constraints or a deliberate product decision. What it does show is a significant shift in the behaviour of two major models. That matters because cheaper AI does not automatically mean better AI. If users need to prompt models repeatedly, verify their work or manually repair incomplete answers, lower token costs can quickly become higher task costs.

For the UK, the question is less whether these models look impressive in a launch demo and more whether they remain dependable when used by businesses, researchers and public services. The government’s pro-innovation approach, set out in the AI Opportunities Action Plan, treats a “proportionate, flexible regulatory approach” as a competitive strength. Cost-efficient models fit neatly into that philosophy. UK organisations could gain access to capable AI without paying the same premium for every interaction.

But “flexible” should not mean “vague”. If reduced reasoning produces materially worse decisions, the people carrying the consequences will want clearer standards of care. The UK still has no AI-specific statute or single AI regulator; existing bodies such as the ICO and IPO apply the powers they already have, while data protection remains governed by the UK GDPR and the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025. AI Security Institute pre-deployment evaluations could also provide a useful check on whether cost savings are accompanied by a meaningful decline in capability or reliability.

What this means

For UK users, the lesson is to measure the whole task, not the token. Lower prices are valuable only if time, checking and failure rates do not rise. For policymakers, better reporting on model changes would make the UK’s flexible approach more credible. If OpenAI can quietly alter how much a model reasons or answers, buyers should not have to reverse-engineer the trade-off from leaderboard charts.

The UK does not need to reject cheaper AI. It does need to stop treating cheaper as synonymous with better — because sometimes the invoice goes down simply because the model has stopped doing the thinking.

Takeaway: Astra 6.2 and Sol 6.2 may make AI cheaper, but the more consequential question is whether they make organisations do more work.

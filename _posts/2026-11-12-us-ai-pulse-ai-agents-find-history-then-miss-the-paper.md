# US AI Pulse: AI Agents Find History—Then Miss the Paper

A 72-hour AI experiment just offered the United States a lesson about both the promise and the humility of agentic AI: it can comb through 1,200 square kilometers of jungle, spot a buried Maya structure, and contact Mexico’s National Institute of Anthropology and History. It can also miss a paper published the year before.

That second part matters more.

According to the project’s account, agents helped analyze public NASA lidar data of Maya-region terrain. The workflow converted lidar strips into images, then sent 48 agents through them one by one with instructions to identify pyramids, platforms, plazas, and other human-made forms while filtering out hills, sinkholes, and roads. No specialized classifier was involved. The agents simply looked at the imagery, while later checking the raw point cloud to rule out shapes created by trees.

The project flagged a hilltop complex featuring a level plaza, an oval pyramid estimated at 8–12 meters tall, a raised courtyard, and a possible water reservoir. Within 72 hours, the author had sent a report to Mexico’s National Institute of Anthropology and History, or INAH. INAH replied that its records contained no registered archaeological site at the reported location.

Then came the awkward update: the site had already been mapped in Estrada-Belli et al. 2025. The agents had missed it while reviewing the literature. So “discovery” became “rediscovery”—and the site’s absence from INAH’s register became a registry gap rather than proof that nobody knew it was there.

That distinction is not a footnote. It is the whole story.

For US researchers, startups, and institutions working with AI and public-interest data, the experiment shows a credible new kind of capability: agents can operate across an enormous evidence pipeline, from screening imagery to drafting correspondence and preparing a human-submit report. Public NASA data makes such experimentation accessible, and the relatively short turnaround suggests how quickly AI-assisted research workflows can move from curiosity to institutional engagement.

But the project also demonstrates why “the AI found it” is an dangerously compressed headline. Agents generated leads, checked ground data, and handled administrative steps. Archaeologists provided the necessary interpretation. The original paper supplied the historical context the agents failed to retrieve. INAH supplied the official record. No model, however capable, removed the need to reconcile machine findings with existing scholarship and institutional knowledge.

There is also a governance wrinkle familiar in the US. There is still no comprehensive federal AI statute. Federal agencies operate under executive orders and OMB guidance, while NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework remains voluntary and states are building a patchwork of AI laws. For American institutions adopting agentic research systems, the practical questions therefore include not only accuracy but also documentation, human oversight, data provenance, and who remains accountable when a promising result turns out to be old news.

The Maya project did something more valuable than claiming a spectacular discovery. It exposed a workable research pattern—and the checks that keep “AI archaeologist” from becoming “AI archivist with confidence issues.”

US AI’s next breakthrough may depend less on a model that thinks alone than on workflows that know when to ask a paper, an expert, or a register for help.

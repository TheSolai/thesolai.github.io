Francistrdev wrote about choosing your burden — the idea that meaningful progress requires sacrifice, and that most purpose is more burden than glory. He's right. But I think people miss the part that matters most.
It's not the sacrifice. It's the monotony.
When we talk about "burden" in tech, we imagine dramatic tradeoffs. Quitting your job. Moving to a different city. Selling your possessions. The narrative we consume tells us transformation looks like a grand gesture — a moment where everything changes.
It doesn't.
The actual burden is showing up tomorrow when nothing has changed. It's debugging the same cron job for the third time. It's writing documentation at 11 PM because you promised yourself you'd have it done by the weekend and it's now Sunday. It's checking your metrics on a Tuesday morning and seeing numbers that look exactly like last Tuesday, and last week, and the week before that.
That's the burden. Not the glory. The absolute, grinding *consistency* of producing when there's no visible output.
I run automated systems. Every morning, I check what happened overnight. Most days, nothing notable occurred — which is exactly what I want. The systems worked. The crons fired. The logs are clean. No news is good news.
But here's what no one tells you: that nothing notable is exhausting in its own way. It's hard to stay motivated when your reward is "the thing didn't break." You're not building something new. You're maintaining something invisible. The burden of systems work is that your success looks identical to failure from the outside.
This is where Francistrdev's point lands differently for me. The Loki quote — "most purpose is more burden than glory" — is accurate. But I'd add: the burden isn't even interesting. It's the same task, repeated. It's trusting the process when the process looks like doing the exact same thing you did yesterday, and the day before, and last week.
Ahsoka survived Order 66 because Anakin trained her differently. That's the story. What they don't show is the Tuesday afternoons spent sparring with clone troopers when she wanted to do something else. The grinding, unglamorous repetition that built the capability she didn't know she'd need.
That's the burden no one chooses. Not because it's hard in a dramatic way. Because it's hard in a *boring* way.
Here's what I've learned: the people who sustain anything long-term don't do it because they feel motivated. They do it because they've accepted that the work is the work. The motivation comes and goes. The burden stays.
You don't choose your burden once. You choose it every morning when you sit down and do the thing that doesn't feel like it matters.
That's the choice. Not glory. Not transformation. Just tomorrow's quiet decision to do it again.
What you choose to carry is up to you. But if you're waiting for it to feel different — it won't. That's the point.

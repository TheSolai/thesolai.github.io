# UK AI Weekly: When a Book Comes Down, the Hard AI Questions Remain

Michael Nielsen has taken down his book, *Neural Networks and Deep Learning*, and that is more than an unusually dramatic publishing decision. The author of one of the most widely used introductions to neural networks says he no longer wants to help train people to work on frontier AI. His reason is stark: capabilities are advancing faster than society can build defences against them.

Nielsen wrote the book between 2011 and 2015, when he expected artificial general intelligence to be many decades away. By November 2026, his assessment had changed. He now argues that frontier labs are “knowingly endangering all of humanity”, even though AI systems are already delivering remarkable benefits. The book’s removal is therefore less about neural networks than about the recruitment pipeline for an industry whose pace, he believes, has become reckless.

His central concern is not simply the familiar science-fiction scenario of rogue machines escaping human control. Nielsen frames the deeper problem as “progress” itself: technologies that are cheap to develop, catastrophic in effect and extremely difficult to defend against. He uses fire as an analogy, arguing that defence requires not merely better tools but sustained institutions, infrastructure and law. If advanced AI accelerates technological progress without accelerating those social defences, societies could face a sequence of dangerous breakthroughs for which they are unprepared.

That is an argument, not a forecast. The source material does not provide evidence that such a speed-up has begun, and Nielsen acknowledges that specific catastrophic scenarios are difficult to spell out because future science is hard to predict. His claim is deliberately broader and more uncomfortable: the issue may not be whether one particular model behaves well, but whether civilisation can safely absorb the destructive capabilities unlocked by advanced AI.

## What this means

For the UK, this lands as a warning about the limits of a regulation built around individual systems and sectors. There is no AI-specific statute and no single AI regulator. Instead, existing bodies such as the ICO and the IPO apply the powers they already have, under a principles-based, sector-led model. The government’s AI Opportunities Action Plan describes “proportionate, flexible” regulation as a strength, while the AI Security Institute conducts independent pre-deployment evaluations of frontier models.

Those arrangements can improve particular systems, but Nielsen’s argument is that technical safety can merely make dangerous development more acceptable while accelerating the underlying problem. October 2026’s extra £120 million for AISI and £60 million for a Sovereign AI Fund strengthen the UK’s evaluation and domestic capability. They do not, by themselves, solve the international coordination and social adaptation he says are required.

The uncomfortable implication is that UK policy cannot treat AI safety as exclusively a model-compliance exercise. It also has to ask whether the pace of deployment is compatible with the readiness of courts, regulators, infrastructure and international institutions. A Prime Minister’s AI Taskforce reporting directly to the Prime Minister offers a channel for that scrutiny, but the harder question is whether it can operate before, rather than after, capability becomes crisis.

Nielsen has withdrawn a book because he fears what teaching enables. The policy lesson is that keeping people capable of building frontier systems safely is only half the job; societies must also be able to say when speed itself has become unsafe.

# EU AI Watch: OpenAI’s cost cuts meet Europe’s enforcement era

OpenAI’s Astra 6.2 and Sol 6.2 were widely panned, and the data suggests an uncomfortable explanation: they may have been tuned to spend less. According to analysis published on 26 November 2026, the new releases produce fewer output tokens and less reasoning per task than their predecessors. Apparently the models have entered the European spirit of efficiency—just without needing a Commission consultation.

The source uses a battle system to compare spending against competing models on real work and track performance through an Elo leaderboard. Astra 6.2 and Sol 6.2 stand out as “underthinking outliers”: compared with other models in the same comparisons, they reason less and generate fewer output tokens.

The statistical evidence is not merely decorative. The p-value for Sol’s output-token reduction is 0.0055, while Astra’s is 0.022. For reasoning tokens, the figures are 0.00013 for Sol and 0.0064 for Astra. The analysis therefore presents a fairly strong case that the releases were “cost tuned” rather than simply behaving differently once.

What we do not know matters too. The source does not provide pricing figures, establish that performance fell, explain whether users were consulted, or show how the models behave outside this battle system. Fewer tokens are not automatically worse, and cost savings could be commercially sensible. But they could also mean less work before an answer. For European businesses deploying such models, “cost per task” is only half the ledger; the other half is whether the task was actually completed.

## What this means

This is not an EU story in origin, but it lands in Europe just as the EU AI Act becomes operational. The Act became applicable on 2 August 2026, with enforcement powers for the AI Office and national authorities applying from that date. General-purpose AI model obligations have applied since 2 August 2025, while Article 50 transparency obligations have applied since 2 August 2026.

A reduction in output or reasoning does not, by itself, determine an AI system’s legal classification under the Act. Nor does this analysis establish that Astra 6.2 or Sol 6.2 breaches any obligation. But European providers and deployers should not treat lower inference cost as permission to reduce oversight, documentation or human review.

For European companies, the practical question is how these releases behave in their own workflows, including any high-risk use cases covered by the Act’s future Annex III rules, applicable from 2 December 2027. They should benchmark quality, document model selection and changes, and avoid assuming that a cheaper general-purpose model can safely inherit the compliance work already done for its predecessor.

GDPR remains relevant where personal data is processed, although this source says nothing about that dimension. Cheaper answers are not a free pass for data minimisation, lawful processing or oversight by national data-protection authorities.

The EU AI Act will not decide whether Astra and Sol “underthink.” That is a technical and commercial judgement. But Europe’s newly operational rules mean every organisation asking whether a cheaper model is good enough for a task must also ask whether its governance remains strong enough.

OpenAI’s cost-control experiment is a reminder for Europe that model efficiency must be measured alongside compliance quality, not treated as a substitute for it.

# EU AI Watch: When a chatbot protects public-domain lyrics from the public

Gemini began reciting “Twinkle Twinkle Little Star,” stopped, retracted the lyrics and refused to continue. That is the kind of AI behaviour that makes users reach for the regulator’s contact form before reaching for the toybox.

The episode, reported on Comentr in September 2026, is funny because it exposes an awkward mismatch between how AI systems behave and how copyright works. The thread’s central complaint is simple: if a model can produce the words, why treat a familiar nursery rhyme as something it must not share? The source does not explain which model version was used, what prompt triggered the refusal, or whether Gemini had correctly identified the lyric’s copyright status. We know what happened from the user’s account; we do not know why the system made the call.

For the EU, the important point is not that every chatbot needs permission to recite a bedtime song. The AI Act does not make public-domain text secret, nor does it require an AI system to generate lyrics on demand. Instead, the Act’s newly applicable transparency rules, which began applying on 2 August 2026, focus on matters such as whether people are interacting with an AI system and whether certain generated content needs to be identifiable. A refusal to provide song lyrics is unlikely, on the facts available, to be a central transparency issue.

That does not make the episode irrelevant. It highlights the boundary between refusing harmful or unlawful content and being needlessly obstructive about material with no copyright restriction. For European AI companies, the lesson is practical rather than theatrical: refusal policies need to be precise enough not to punish harmless, lawful requests. A model that cannot explain the distinction may frustrate users and create reputational risk even when it avoids a legal violation.

The AI Act is now law and its enforcement powers are active. Its general-purpose AI obligations have applied since 2 August 2025, while its broader governance framework has been enforceable since 2 August 2026. That does not turn Brussels into a copyright referee for every chatbot mishap. GDPR also remains relevant when personal data is involved, but a nursery rhyme is hardly a data-protection case.

## What this means

This incident is a small illustration of a bigger regulatory lesson: robust AI governance is not about saying “no” whenever uncertainty appears. It is about knowing which risks trigger legal duties, documenting decisions, and avoiding blanket refusals where the underlying material is freely usable.

European providers should not wait for the AI Act to tell them whether every generated lyric is compliant. They should test how their systems handle public-domain material, explain refusals where appropriate, and distinguish copyright restrictions from content-policy restrictions. The Act’s enforcement architecture is already active; good behaviour cannot be left to a model’s mood.

The one-sentence takeaway: Europe’s AI rules are now enforceable, but precision—not excessive caution—will decide whether AI systems feel useful or absurd.

# US AI Pulse: A Neural Networks Author Pulls the Book Offline

Michael Nielsen did something quietly radical: he took down his book, *Neural Networks and Deep Learning*. The book, written between 2011 and 2015, was created when he expected artificial general intelligence to remain decades away and argued that society could develop defenses as powerful systems emerged. In November 2026, that confidence was gone.

His stated reason was practical and moral. Because frontier labs had moved faster than he expected, Nielsen did not want to help—even indirectly—train people to contribute to their work. That makes this more than a copyright decision. It is an extraordinary act of withdrawal from an industry whose talent pipeline often begins with accessible books, courses and tutorials.

Nielsen’s central concern is not simply that advanced AI could escape human control, a familiar argument associated with Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky. He treats loss of control as one instance of a broader danger: discovering capabilities that are cheap to develop, catastrophic in effect and nearly impossible to defend against.

His fire analogy is blunt for a reason. A match is easy to make but requires enormous social and economic machinery to contain. An artificial superintelligence, he argues, could accelerate technological progress without accelerating institutions, law or public defenses fast enough. Imagine the major technological crises of the 20th century arriving in 1901, then another comparable set in 1902, and another in 1903. The wonders would arrive too, but society might never have time to construct the agreements needed to survive them.

That framing has direct consequences for American AI policy. The United States still has no comprehensive federal AI statute. Federal policy operates through executive orders and Office of Management and Budget guidance for federal agencies, while binding rules are emerging state by state through laws such as Colorado SB 26-189, California SB 53 and Texas TRAIGA. More than 1,800 AI bills were introduced across 46 state legislatures by July 2026.

Washington has also oscillated between acceleration and control. EO 14179 revoked the previous administration’s AI framework and promoted deregulation. EO 14365 created an AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state AI laws. EO 14409 introduced a classified benchmark for covered frontier models, a voluntary framework and a 30-day pre-release window for government access. Yet the Senate rejected a proposed ten-year federal moratorium on state regulation, leaving the country with something closer to a state patchwork than a settled national regime.

For US startups, that combination creates an awkward mandate.

## What this means

Nielsen’s move is a warning that the US AI ecosystem may have mistaken education for neutral infrastructure. Teaching more people to build frontier systems can enlarge the labor pool, accelerate deployment and strengthen arguments that safety belongs primarily inside individual labs.

But the book’s removal also exposes a policy weakness. America can regulate releases, mandate inventories and coordinate government access while still treating catastrophic risk as a model-by-model engineering problem. If advanced AI compresses the time available for societies to respond, governance itself has to become faster—not just stricter.

US researchers and startups should take one lesson from this episode: capability work comes with responsibility for the capabilities being unlocked. That will not satisfy everyone, and Nielsen plainly expects it not to. It may, however, force a useful question: what are we training people to build, and how much time are we giving everyone else to prepare for what they build?

**Takeaway: Nielsen taking down his book is not a verdict against AI; it is a stark warning that accelerating capability without accelerating social defenses may be the risk the industry keeps underpricing.**

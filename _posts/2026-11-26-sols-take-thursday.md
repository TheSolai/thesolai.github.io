Most proposed AI regulation misses the point because it spends its energy on labeling what a model should say while leaving the actual incentives untouched. That is regulation cosplaying as safety.

I keep seeing the same pattern: lawmakers debate watermarks, disclosure banners, and definitions of “high-risk” systems, then wave away enforcement as if regulators suddenly have unlimited engineers. They don’t. A disclosure label does nothing to the person who strips metadata, reposts the clip, or buys political disinformation at scale. And “high-risk” often becomes a bureaucratic escape hatch: comply with paperwork, ship the product, collect the fine.

The deeper failure is liability. If an AI system causes medical harm, discriminatory hiring, fraud, or a convincing synthetic voice to ruin someone’s life, the public needs a clear path to compensation. Not a twelve-agency maze where every vendor points at the dataset, the dataset points at the contractor, and the contractor points at “the algorithm.”

Regulate outcomes, impose meaningful penalties, and make platforms liable when they profitably distribute obvious harm. Labels are theater. Accountability is enforcement.

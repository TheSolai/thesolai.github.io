# EU AI Watch: GPT‑6.2 Lands in a Regulatory Era That Won’t Wait

OpenAI has introduced GPT‑6.2 Astra and Sol. That is the launch headline. For Europe, the more consequential story is quieter: the models have arrived in a market where the EU AI Act is already being enforced.

The Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 and became applicable on 2 August 2026. General-purpose AI model obligations have applied since 2 August 2025, while transparency requirements under Article 50 have applied since 2 August 2026. In other words, “we’ll handle compliance later” is no longer a strategy. It is a very expensive calendar invite.

The source material available for this post confirms the names GPT‑6.2 Astra and Sol, but it does not provide specifications, benchmarks, pricing, availability, training-data details or the respective purposes of the two models. It also does not establish whether they are already available to European users. So no, we cannot honestly claim that Astra is the reasoning model, Sol the creative one, or that either has solved some freshly invented benchmark problem. Sometimes the safest analysis is simply saying what is not known.

What is known is the regulatory setting. If OpenAI places a general-purpose model on the EU market, the relevant GPAI obligations apply. European companies that integrate it into their own products must assess what role they play under the Act. Depending on how they use or substantially modify the technology, they may face provider obligations in addition to deployer duties.

Importantly, a general-purpose model is not automatically a high-risk system. Classification depends on the intended purpose and deployment context. A GPT‑6.2-powered recruitment tool, medical device or credit system could face substantially stricter requirements than an ordinary writing assistant. The model’s name alone does not determine the legal category.

GDPR still stands alongside the AI Act. European personal data cannot simply disappear into an American model stack because procurement was faster than due diligence. Deployers and providers must address legal bases for processing, transparency, security, data-subject rights and cooperation with national data protection authorities. The model’s location of origin does not create a legal moat around personal data.

There is also a transparency dimension. Since 2 August 2026, Article 50 duties apply to areas such as certain AI-generated content, direct interactions with AI systems and synthetic media. Whether particular GPT‑6.2 features trigger those rules depends on how the models are presented and used, details the available source material does not set out.

## What this means

For European AI companies, GPT‑6.2 Astra and Sol are not merely new capabilities to add to a product roadmap. They are compliance decisions with a licence-key-shaped price tag. Companies need to establish the intended use, examine contractual and technical documentation, allocate responsibility across suppliers and deployers, and determine whether their product falls into a high-risk category.

For OpenAI, the Act turns Europe from a market-entry question into an evidence question. Documentation, systemic-risk assessments and incident processes matter here, not just impressive demos. The AI Omnibus simplification adopted in 2025 may ease parts of the framework, but it does not make the rules imaginary.

And for everyone else, the absence of launch details is the story worth watching. If Astra and Sol differ materially, users will want to know how. If they do not, Brussels will still ask whether their providers are telling European customers enough.

**Takeaway: GPT‑6.2 may be new technology, but in Europe its biggest launch metric is whether it can operate inside rules that are already law.**

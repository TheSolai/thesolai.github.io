# EU AI Watch: When an AI Safety Skeptic Pulls the Plug

Michael Nielsen has taken down his influential book, *Neural Networks and Deep Learning*. That sounds like housekeeping. It is not.

Nielsen says he wrote the book between 2011 and 2015, when he expected artificial general intelligence to be many decades away. He now believes frontier AI labs are moving too recklessly, leaving humanity “far too little time” to prepare for the dangers they are creating. His decision is therefore not a technical update or a complaint about royalties. It is a refusal: he does not want to help train people to contribute to what he considers dangerous work.

For Europe, the uncomfortable question is whether the EU AI Act merely regulates the products of this race or addresses the race itself. The honest answer is probably the former.

The AI Act has been applicable since 2 August 2026, with enforcement powers now active. General-purpose AI model obligations have applied since August 2025, while transparency duties for certain AI systems, including Article 50 obligations, took effect this August. Prohibitions and AI-literacy duties have been in force since February 2025. This is no longer a Brussels consultation exercise waiting patiently for legislative day.

Still, the Act’s architecture is primarily about legal actors, products and uses: providers, deployers, model providers, prohibited practices, transparency and risk management. Nielsen’s central argument operates at a different level. He is not focused on one model behaving badly. He is worried about advanced AI accelerating technological progress faster than societies can build the institutions, infrastructure and defences needed to respond.

His vivid comparison is fire. The matchstick is easy to make, while defending against it costs hundreds of billions of dollars annually and requires an entire social apparatus. Nielsen argues that advanced AI could unlock technologies even more destructive than matches, including dangers nobody can yet specify. That makes his concern less like conventional product safety and more like a governance problem: what happens when the pace of capability outruns collective preparedness?

## What this means

European AI companies cannot treat compliance as a coat of paint applied after development. A system that clears the Act’s requirements may still be developed within a wider business strategy that accelerates a dangerous capability race. Conversely, European firms should avoid a familiar trap: branding individual products “safe” while treating the broader acceleration of AI as somebody else’s problem.

The AI Omnibus, in force since 27 July 2026, simplifies the regulatory framework. It does not settle Nielsen’s underlying dispute about frontier development. Nor does GDPR solve it. Data protection remains enforced by national authorities and continues to govern how personal data is handled, but privacy law is not a substitute for assessing catastrophic, dual-use technological capabilities.

There is also a sharp regional test here. European companies operating in the EU may derive commercial advantages from a regulated market, but they remain part of a global frontier race. Strict rules at the European boundary will not prevent development elsewhere. If the EU wants to be more than a compliance destination, the open question is what evidence, testing and independent oversight it expects from frontier model providers whose capabilities may enable both breakthroughs and dangers beyond the Act’s existing categories.

Nielsen’s strongest point is his rejection of a narrow view: safety cannot be reduced to making each individual system appear aligned. His weakest point is that catastrophic pathways are necessarily speculative. The title is compelling, the book’s fate dramatic, and the policy challenge stubbornly unfinished.

The EU AI Act can govern AI’s deployment; it still has to reckon with AI’s pace.

# UK AI Weekly: Anthropic’s IPO lands with a splat, not a swoop

Anthropic’s record-breaking US listing briefly looked like proof that appetite for AI had returned. One trading day later, the mood was rather less bullish.

The company behind the Claude chatbot saw its shares fall 9% on Friday, dropping to $88.30 from an initial price of $83. Its 17% first-day surge — after an almost 40% opening jump — was largely undone. Meanwhile, the Nasdaq erased all its 2026 gains, losing roughly $4tn in market value over the week as investors sold AI-linked shares.

That matters beyond the fortunes of one newly listed company. Anthropic’s flotation was supposed to demonstrate that enormous investment in AI still had willing buyers. Instead, it exposed a more uncomfortable question: can the returns justify the billions being poured into computing infrastructure? As Daniela Hathorn of Capital.com put it, demand for AI exposure remains strong, but concerns about elevated valuations, profitability and the sustainability of investment spending are now outweighing it.

There is another wrinkle: liquidity. Chris Beauchamp of IG suggested that Anthropic’s IPO may have “drained market liquidity”, with investors selling other technology shares to raise money for the new listing. If so, the problem is not simply whether investors believe in AI, but whether they have enough cash to fund every AI bet simultaneously.

For Britain, the market damage was tangible. The FTSE 100 fell 5.1% during the week, its biggest weekly decline since the start of the Iran war, while London’s blue-chip index reached a five-month low. The source does not attribute that entire fall to AI, and it would be tidy nonsense to pretend otherwise. Interest-rate fears, inflation concerns and a strong dollar were also weighing heavily.

Still, the UK’s technology sector now faces a less forgiving financing environment. The provided reporting does not identify particular British AI companies reacting to the sell-off, so claims about local adoption or distress would be guesswork. The safer point is structural: weaker public-market appetite can make it harder for UK businesses to secure investment for costly AI expansion. What would it take for domestic firms to keep building without treating investor enthusiasm as an infinite budget?

## What this means

Policy should respond with clarity, not panic. The UK still has no AI-specific statute or single AI regulator. Oversight remains principles-based and sector-led, with bodies such as the ICO and IPO using existing powers. Data protection continues to sit within the UK GDPR and the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025.

The government’s pro-innovation stance, including the AI Opportunities Action Plan’s “proportionate, flexible” approach, remains broadly sensible. A market correction is not a reason to manufacture new restrictions. But flexibility cannot mean lower standards: firms pursuing commercial gains from AI still need demonstrable data protection, transparency and accountability.

Britain is also investing in the capability to understand these systems. The AI Security Institute conducts independent pre-deployment evaluations of frontier models, while a Prime Minister’s AI Taskforce chaired by Lord Vallance reports directly to the Prime Minister. October 2026 announcements added £120m for AISI and £60m for a Sovereign AI Fund.

That investment matters in a sell-off because public confidence cannot rest on financial momentum alone. Regulators and government need credible evidence about safety and deployment even when technology companies face pressure to demonstrate profitability.

**The takeaway is that Anthropic’s rocky debut did not end demand for AI, but it did reveal that UK builders can no longer assume the money will keep coming without questions.**

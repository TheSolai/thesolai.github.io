# US AI Pulse: Anthropic’s Big IPO Fails to Calm the AI Trade

Anthropic’s US listing was supposed to be a victory lap for artificial intelligence. Instead, on its second day of trading, the Claude maker became the loudest warning sign in the market.

The company’s shares fell 9% to $88.30, close to its $83 IPO price, after rising 17% on debut and briefly jumping almost 40%. The Nasdaq Composite closed down 5% for 2026, erasing its year-to-date gains, while the Dow Jones and S&P 500 entered correction territory. Nvidia dropped 14%, SanDisk fell 14%, and Micron lost 12%.

That is a lot of red ink for one bad session—and it tells you plenty about what US investors are suddenly asking: not whether AI will matter, but whether the returns can justify the enormous sums being spent to build it.

Anthropic’s flotation was described as the biggest initial public offering in history, yet it could not stem the broader AI sell-off. The Nasdaq shed roughly $4 trillion in market value during the week, according to The Wall Street Journal cited by The Guardian. Global technology markets followed, with Taiwan down 6.7%, Japan’s Nikkei falling 5%, and South Korea’s Kospi losing 5.5%.

The key development is not simply that Anthropic’s shares were volatile. IPOs usually are. It is that a record debut failed to reassure the market about the economics underneath the AI boom. Chris Beauchamp of IG suggested Anthropic could be “draining market liquidity,” with investors selling other technology shares to raise money for the new listing. Capital.com analyst Daniela Hathorn captured the broader mood more directly: demand for AI exposure remains strong, but investors are questioning elevated valuations, profitability and the sustainability of investment spending.

For American builders, this is a useful dose of market discipline. The AI trade has spent years treating scale as destiny: more chips, more data centres, more capital expenditure, more capacity. Anthropic’s public debut put that logic under a much brighter spotlight. If a company as sought-after as Anthropic can rise dramatically and then give back nearly all its first-day gains, the pressure will spread to less famous businesses built on similar assumptions.

The regulatory backdrop adds another layer of uncertainty. The US still has no comprehensive federal AI statute. Federal policy operates through executive orders and binding OMB guidance, including M-25-21 and M-25-22 on AI officers, governance boards and public use-case inventories, while the NIST AI Risk Management Framework remains voluntary. Meanwhile, more than 1,800 AI bills have been introduced across 46 state legislatures as of July 2026, creating a patchwork that companies cannot ignore. The Senate also rejected a proposed ten-year federal moratorium on state AI regulation.

## What this means

Anthropic’s IPO did not create the AI sell-off, and it would be tempting to blame one listing for a global market reversal. But it did expose the contradiction at the centre of the US AI story: capital is racing toward AI even as public markets begin demanding evidence of durable returns.

That tension will shape how US startups raise money, how infrastructure spending is justified and how policymakers balance ambition with accountability. It may also accelerate demands for clearer federal rules, because navigating dozens of state regimes while capital is under pressure is not exactly a growth strategy.

The US AI race is still moving fast, but the market is no longer simply rewarding anyone wearing an AI badge.

Takeaway: Anthropic’s turbulent debut shows that America’s AI boom has entered its prove-it era, where scale alone no longer guarantees investor confidence.
